# §1.4.1 Eccentric-anomaly sampling and ISOFIT-style joint harmonics

The classical Jedrzejewski (1987) algorithm samples the trial
ellipse uniformly in the image position angle $\phi$ and decomposes
the resulting intensity profile in the same $\phi$-basis. This is a
natural choice for low-ellipticity isophotes, where uniform-in-$\phi$
sampling is approximately uniform-in-arclength and the harmonic
basis is approximately orthogonal with respect to the path on the
ellipse. It fails — sometimes severely — when the isophotes
themselves are highly elliptical, as they are in edge-on disks,
X-shaped / peanut-bulge galaxies, and strongly barred systems where
the bar component dominates a range of SMAs. ISOSTER provides two
related features that together extend the classical algorithm into
this regime: *eccentric-anomaly sampling* (Ciambur 2015), which
replaces $\phi$ with the eccentric anomaly $\psi$ as the angular
basis; and *ISOFIT-style joint harmonics*, which fits higher-order
deviation coefficients jointly with the geometry harmonics inside
the iteration loop rather than as a post-hoc add-on. Both can be
used independently; together they recover the Ciambur (2015)
formulation faithfully within the ISOSTER framework.

## §1.4.1.1 Why uniform-in-$\phi$ sampling fails at high ellipticity

Consider an elliptical isophote with semi-major axis $a$,
ellipticity $\varepsilon$, and semi-minor axis $b = a(1 -
\varepsilon)$. Parametrize the ellipse by the image position angle
$\phi$ measured from the major axis. The radial distance from the
ellipse center to the boundary at angle $\phi$ is

$$
r(\phi) = \frac{b}{\sqrt{1 - \varepsilon(2 - \varepsilon)\cos^2\phi}},
$$

and the arc-length increment per unit $\phi$ is

$$
\frac{ds}{d\phi} = \sqrt{r^2(\phi) + \left(\frac{dr}{d\phi}\right)^2}.
$$

For $\varepsilon \to 0$ (a circle), this is constant and uniform
$\phi$-sampling is uniform in arc length. For $\varepsilon$
approaching unity, $ds / d\phi$ varies by orders of magnitude over
the ellipse perimeter: it is large near $\phi = 0$ (along the major
axis, where the path moves quickly outward in image coordinates per
unit $\phi$) and small near $\phi = \pi / 2$ (along the minor axis,
where the path doubles back tightly). The consequence is that
uniform-in-$\phi$ sampling *under-samples* the major-axis region —
exactly the region where edge-on disks and peanut bulges have their
most distinctive isophotal structure — and *over-samples* the
minor-axis region, where the path is shortest and the structural
information per sample is least.

The under-sampling produces two related problems. First, the
harmonic basis $\{\sin n\phi, \cos n\phi\}$ is no longer orthogonal
with respect to arc length on the highly elliptical isophote, so a
geometrically perfect ellipse — one whose intensity is constant
along the ellipse — produces *nonzero* higher-order coefficients in
the $\phi$-basis when fit by ordinary least squares with uniform
weights. The coefficients $A_n$, $B_n$ for $n \ge 3$ are therefore
contaminated by a sampling-density artefact whose amplitude grows
with ellipticity. Distinguishing this artefact from the genuine
boxy / disky / peanut-bulge structure that the higher-order
coefficients are designed to measure requires either an
ellipticity-dependent correction (Ciambur 2015 §3) or a change of
sampling basis.

Second, geometry-mismatch terms in orders 1 and 2 are also
distorted, because the dominant amplitude
$a_\max = \max(|A_1|, |B_1|, |A_2|, |B_2|)$ used to select the
geometry-update direction is computed in the same biased basis.
The convergence criterion still works — the iteration drives
$a_\max$ toward zero — but the converged geometry inherits a
sampling-density bias that an ellipticity-aware basis avoids.

## §1.4.1.2 The eccentric-anomaly parametrization

The eccentric anomaly $\psi$ is the angular parameter that runs
uniformly along the ellipse when the latter is mapped to an
auxiliary unit circle. In the ellipse's principal coordinate system
(major axis along $x'$, minor along $y'$), the ellipse is the image
of the unit circle under the affine map
$(x', y') = (a\cos\psi, b\sin\psi)$. The arc-length increment per
unit $\psi$ is

$$
\frac{ds}{d\psi} = \sqrt{a^2 \sin^2\psi + b^2 \cos^2\psi},
$$

which varies between $b$ and $a$ over a full cycle but never
vanishes and is much closer to uniform than $ds / d\phi$ at any
fixed ellipticity. In the limit $\varepsilon \to 1$,
$ds / d\psi$ ranges over a factor of $a / b$ rather than the
$(a / b)^2$ range that $ds / d\phi$ takes — a quadratic
improvement in sampling uniformity.

The relationship between $\psi$ and the polar angle $\theta$
measured from the ellipse center (the true anomaly, not the image
position angle $\phi$ used elsewhere in this section) is

$$
\tan\theta = \frac{b}{a}\tan\psi = (1 - \varepsilon)\tan\psi.
$$

ISOSTER computes ellipse-boundary coordinates by stepping uniformly
through $\psi \in [0, 2\pi)$, evaluating $(x', y')$ on the principal
axes via the closed-form mapping, then rotating into image
coordinates by the position angle PA. The sampling step in
`isoster/sampling.py` is the same vectorized
`scipy.ndimage.map_coordinates` call described in §1.3.2; only the
coordinate construction differs. The harmonic fit is then performed
in the $\psi$-basis: the design matrix becomes

$$
\mathbf{A}_\psi = [\mathbf{1}, \sin\psi, \cos\psi, \sin 2\psi, \cos 2\psi,
\sin n_1\psi, \cos n_1\psi, \ldots],
$$

and the coefficients $A_n^\psi$, $B_n^\psi$ measure intrinsic
isophotal shape directly, without the ellipticity-dependent
sampling-density contamination. For a perfect ellipse sampled
uniformly in $\psi$ and fit in the $\psi$-basis, the higher-order
coefficients vanish exactly to within fit noise — which is the
property the harmonic decomposition is supposed to have but loses
in the $\phi$-basis at high ellipticity.

One subtlety bears emphasizing. The eccentric anomaly is defined
relative to the *current trial geometry* — its definition depends
on the trial $\varepsilon$ and PA. As the iteration updates the
geometry, the mapping $\psi \to (x_\mathrm{image},
y_\mathrm{image})$ also updates. This is the right behaviour for
the harmonic-fit basis but is the wrong behaviour for the
geometry-update step: a position-angle shift of $\Delta\mathrm{PA}$
in $\psi$-coordinates does not correspond to the same image-frame
shift it does in $\phi$-coordinates, so updating PA in $\psi$-space
would produce a self-referential drift. ISOSTER avoids this by
keeping the *geometry update* in $\phi$-space — the harmonic
coefficients $(A_1, B_1, A_2, B_2)$ in the $\psi$-basis are
converted back to $\phi$-space update directions before they touch
$(x_0, y_0, \varepsilon, \mathrm{PA})$. The user-visible config flag
`use_eccentric_anomaly = True` therefore changes *only* the harmonic
basis; the geometry parameters retain their classical geometric
meaning, and the converged $(x_0, y_0, \varepsilon, \mathrm{PA})$
are directly comparable to a $\phi$-mode fit of the same image.

## §1.4.1.3 ISOFIT-style joint harmonics

The default ISOSTER fit (and the classical Jedrzejewski / photutils
fit) restricts the inside-loop harmonic basis to orders 1 and 2:

$$
\mathbf{A}_5 = [\mathbf{1}, \sin\theta, \cos\theta, \sin 2\theta, \cos 2\theta],
$$

where $\theta$ is the angular basis ($\phi$ or $\psi$ depending on
the sampling mode). Higher-order coefficients $(A_n, B_n)$ for
$n \ge 3$ are fitted *post-hoc* on the converged geometry by
evaluating the same five-parameter fit with the geometry harmonics
fixed and the higher-order coefficients added to the design matrix.
This post-hoc approach has two advantages: the inside-loop solve is
cheap (the design matrix has only five columns); and the
convergence criterion is decoupled from how many higher orders the
user wants to measure.

It has one disadvantage. The higher-order coefficients describe
isophotal shape that is potentially correlated with the geometry
update: a slight error in the converged geometry can leak into the
higher-order coefficients through the cross-correlation of the
basis functions on the sampled intensity profile. For weak
deviations from ellipticity the leakage is negligible, and the
post-hoc approach is the right trade-off. For strong deviations —
X-shaped bulges, peanut bulges, strongly disky / boxy isophotes —
the leakage can be a significant fraction of the higher-order
amplitude itself, and the converged geometry can be biased away
from the geometry that would minimize the *full* harmonic residual
including the higher orders.

ISOFIT (Ciambur 2015) addresses this by performing the joint
inside-loop fit on an *extended* design matrix that includes the
higher-order columns:

$$
\mathbf{A}_\mathrm{ISOFIT} = [\mathbf{1}, \sin\theta, \cos\theta, \sin 2\theta, \cos 2\theta,
\sin n_1\theta, \cos n_1\theta, \ldots, \sin n_L\theta, \cos n_L\theta],
$$

where $\{n_1, \ldots, n_L\}$ are the orders specified in
`harmonic_orders` (default $\{3, 4\}$, with $L = 2$). The
linear-system solve is now $p = 5 + 2L$ parameters wide rather
than $5$, and the cross-correlations between geometry harmonics
and higher-order harmonics are absorbed into the joint covariance
matrix rather than leaked into the geometry update.

ISOSTER provides two ISOFIT variants, both gated by
`simultaneous_harmonics = True`:

- **`isofit_mode = 'in_loop'`** runs the joint solve on every
  iteration of every isophote. This is the most aggressive variant:
  the geometry update at each iteration is computed from the joint
  solve, so the geometry trajectory through iteration space differs
  from the post-hoc fit. The cross-correlations are absorbed
  throughout the iteration, not just at the end.

- **`isofit_mode = 'original'`** runs the classical 5-parameter
  fit inside the loop and applies the joint solve only after
  convergence. This matches the original Ciambur (2015) procedure
  more closely: the inside-loop trajectory is identical to the
  default, but the reported higher-order coefficients are computed
  from the joint solve on the converged geometry. The geometry
  itself is the same as the default post-hoc path; only the
  higher-order coefficients (and their reported uncertainties)
  differ.

In both variants, the extended linear system is solved through
the same NumPy / LAPACK pipeline used in the 5-parameter fit, and
the dominant-coefficient selector for the geometry update
continues to operate on indices 1–4 of the coefficient vector
(corresponding to $A_1, B_1, A_2, B_2$). Coefficient storage in
the output dictionary follows the convention
$[I_0, A_1, B_1, A_2, B_2, A_{n_1}, B_{n_1}, A_{n_2}, B_{n_2}, \ldots]$,
with the $k$-th higher order occupying indices $5 + 2k$ and $5 + 2k + 1$.

## §1.4.1.4 Caveats and fallbacks

Three caveats apply to the ISOFIT path and are worth flagging
explicitly:

**Sample-count fallback.** The extended design matrix requires at
least $2L + 5$ sample points to be well-posed. At small SMAs where
$N$ is at the floor of $\max(64, \lceil 2\pi a \rceil) = 64$, the
condition holds easily for $L = 2$ ($2L + 5 = 9$) but begins to
strain for $L = 4$ or larger when combined with severe masking
(say, more than 80% of the ellipse masked out by a defect or a
nearby source). When the available sample count falls below the
threshold, ISOSTER silently falls back to the 5-parameter fit and
emits a `RuntimeWarning` on the first occurrence per isophote.
The fallback is byte-equivalent to running with
`simultaneous_harmonics = False` for that isophote alone; the rest
of the fit proceeds with the joint solve.

**Compute overhead.** The joint solve adds roughly 25%–35% to the
per-iteration cost of the inside-loop fit, dominated by the cost
of the wider linear system. The exact overhead depends on the
number of orders and the platform's LAPACK implementation; the
benchmark chapters quantify it for typical parameter combinations.
For most science use cases the overhead is dwarfed by the cost of
the image-level outward loop, but it is real and should be
budgeted when running ISOSTER on large samples.

**Geometry-update path unchanged.** Both the `'in_loop'` and the
`'original'` variant update the geometry through the same
coordinate-descent rule used by the default fit: the largest of
$|A_1|, |B_1|, |A_2|, |B_2|$ is identified, and the corresponding
geometry parameter is updated by an amount proportional to the
amplitude scaled by the local radial gradient. The ISOFIT path does
*not* introduce a multi-parameter update step; the geometry
trajectory through iteration space differs from the default only
because the harmonic coefficients themselves differ (their values
absorb cross-correlations with the higher orders). This is
intentional and matches Ciambur (2015) §4: the joint solve
clarifies the values, not the update rule.

A practical point worth mentioning: the combination
`use_eccentric_anomaly = True` *and* `simultaneous_harmonics = True`
with `isofit_mode = 'in_loop'` is the closest faithful
reimplementation of the Ciambur (2015) IRAF `Isofit` task within
the ISOSTER framework. It is the recommended configuration for
edge-on disks, peanut bulges, and strongly barred galaxies where
the higher-order harmonic structure carries the science of
interest, and where the user wants both the sampling-basis
correction and the cross-correlation absorption.

## §1.4.1.5 Demonstration: synthetic edge-on disk

To make the difference visible we use a synthetic edge-on disk
generated through the MockGal renderer in `isophote_test`. The
recipe is a single-component Sersic profile with
$n_\mathrm{Sersic} = 1.0$ (exponential), effective semi-major axis
$R_e = 60\,\mathrm{px}$, ellipticity $\varepsilon = 0.70$
(near-edge-on aspect ratio $a / b \approx 3.3$), position angle
$\mathrm{PA} = 45^\circ$, and total flux scaled so the central
surface brightness is well above the sky noise. The image is
$512 \times 512\,\mathrm{px}$ with Gaussian sky noise corresponding
to $\mu_\mathrm{sky} = 26\,\mathrm{mag\,arcsec^{-2}}$ in a fiducial
band, and the rendered image is convolved with a Gaussian PSF of
FWHM $= 2\,\mathrm{px}$ before noise addition.

fitted isophotes overlaid for the joint EA+ISOFIT mode. Panel B:
the normalized fourth-order coefficient $A_4^\mathrm{norm}(a)$ from
two fits — the default $\phi$-sampling with post-hoc higher
harmonics (red), and the EA+ISOFIT joint fit (blue) — plotted
against semi-major axis. The default-mode curve shows the
characteristic ellipticity-driven rise toward positive
$A_4^\mathrm{norm}$ at large SMAs, a sampling-density artefact;
the EA+ISOFIT curve sits near zero across the same SMA range, as
expected for an intrinsic ellipse. Panel C: the 2-D residual map
(image minus reconstructed model) for the EA+ISOFIT fit, with the
characteristic cross-pattern artefact of the $\phi$-mode fit absent.
Data source: `isophote_test/inputs/demos/` edge-on Sersic mock,
recipe above.]**

The figure is constructed so that the two fits differ in *only* the
sampling basis and the harmonic-fit strategy — the same image, the
same mask, the same starting geometry, the same `astep`, the same
`maxsma` — and the visible difference is therefore attributable to
the choice of basis. Panel B is the cleanest piece of evidence:
the input mock is by construction a pure ellipse with no fourth-order
deviation, so the correct $A_4^\mathrm{norm}$ is zero everywhere; the
default-mode curve departing from zero is the sampling-density
artefact of §1.4.1.1, and the EA+ISOFIT curve sitting at zero is the
correction restoring the expected behaviour.

The reproducibility recipe is:

```python
from isoster import fit_image
from isoster.config import IsosterConfig

# Default fit (phi-sampling, post-hoc higher harmonics)
config_default = IsosterConfig(
    sma0=10.0,
    maxsma=180.0,
    harmonic_orders=[3, 4],
    compute_deviations=True,
)
results_default = fit_image(image, mask=mask, config=config_default)

# EA + ISOFIT joint fit (recommended for edge-on disks)
config_eaisofit = IsosterConfig(
    sma0=10.0,
    maxsma=180.0,
    use_eccentric_anomaly=True,
    simultaneous_harmonics=True,
    isofit_mode="in_loop",
    harmonic_orders=[3, 4],
)
results_eaisofit = fit_image(image, mask=mask, config=config_eaisofit)
```

For the demo above we ran both with the same starting geometry
($x_0 = y_0 = 256\,\mathrm{px}$, $\varepsilon = 0.5$, $\mathrm{PA} =
0$); both converged across the full SMA range. The output
dictionaries can be passed directly to
`isoster.plotting.plot_comparison_qa_figure` to reproduce a
two-method QA panel of the kind shown above.

A final note on when *not* to enable this combination. On
round-ish isophotes ($\varepsilon \lesssim 0.2$) the sampling-density
artefact in the $\phi$-basis is below the level of fit residuals,
and the post-hoc higher-harmonic fit is consistent with the joint
solve. Switching to EA+ISOFIT in that regime adds the 25%–35%
compute overhead without producing scientifically distinguishable
results. The combination is therefore reserved for the regime where
the user has identified strong elliptical or non-elliptical isophote
structure as a science target; on a survey pipeline the simplest
policy is to apply EA+ISOFIT only to galaxies with morphological
classifications or photometric proxies indicating high-ellipticity
or peanut-bulge morphology.
