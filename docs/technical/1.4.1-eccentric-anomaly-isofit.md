# §1.4.1 Eccentric-anomaly sampling and ISOFIT-style joint harmonics

The classical Jedrzejewski (1987) algorithm samples the trial
ellipse uniformly in the image position angle $\phi$ and decomposes
the resulting intensity profile in the same $\phi$-basis. This is a
natural choice for low-ellipticity isophotes, where uniform-in-$\phi$
sampling is approximately uniform-in-arclength and the harmonic
basis is approximately orthogonal with respect to the path on the
ellipse. It fails — sometimes severely — when the isophotes
themselves are highly elliptical, as they are in edge-on disks,
X-shaped / peanut-bulge galaxies, and strongly barred systems where
the bar component dominates a range of SMAs. ISOSTER provides two
related features that together extend the classical algorithm into
this regime: *eccentric-anomaly sampling* (Ciambur 2015), which
replaces $\phi$ with the eccentric anomaly $\psi$ as the angular
basis; and *ISOFIT-style joint harmonics*, which fits higher-order
deviation coefficients jointly with the geometry harmonics inside
the iteration loop rather than as a post-hoc add-on. Both can be
used independently; together they recover the Ciambur (2015)
formulation faithfully within the ISOSTER framework.

## §1.4.1.1 Why uniform-in-$\phi$ sampling fails at high ellipticity

Consider an elliptical isophote with semi-major axis $a$,
ellipticity $\varepsilon$, and semi-minor axis $b = a(1 -
\varepsilon)$. Parametrize the ellipse by the image position angle
$\phi$ measured from the major axis. The radial distance from the
ellipse center to the boundary at angle $\phi$ is

$$
r(\phi) = \frac{b}{\sqrt{1 - \varepsilon(2 - \varepsilon)\cos^2\phi}},
$$

and the arc-length increment per unit $\phi$ is

$$
\frac{ds}{d\phi} = \sqrt{r^2(\phi) + \left(\frac{dr}{d\phi}\right)^2}.
$$

For $\varepsilon \to 0$ (a circle), this is constant and uniform
$\phi$-sampling is uniform in arc length. For $\varepsilon$
approaching unity, $ds / d\phi$ varies by orders of magnitude over
the ellipse perimeter: it is large near $\phi = 0$ (along the major
axis, where the path moves quickly outward in image coordinates per
unit $\phi$) and small near $\phi = \pi / 2$ (along the minor axis,
where the path doubles back tightly). The consequence is that
uniform-in-$\phi$ sampling *under-samples* the major-axis region —
exactly the region where edge-on disks and peanut bulges have their
most distinctive isophotal structure — and *over-samples* the
minor-axis region, where the path is shortest and the structural
information per sample is least.

It is worth being precise about what this does and does not cause,
because the intuitive version of the argument is wrong. Non-uniform
sampling density does *not* manufacture harmonic content out of
nothing: a geometrically perfect ellipse has constant intensity
along its boundary, and a constant is a constant in any angular
coordinate. Fitting $I(\phi) = \mathrm{const}$ in the $\phi$-basis
returns $I_0$ exactly and all harmonic coefficients zero, at any
ellipticity. There is no "sampling-density artefact" that a perfect
ellipse acquires and that a change of basis removes.

The two real problems are different, and both are consequences of
the same fact: $\phi$ and $\psi$ are related by a *nonlinear*
reparametrization, $\tan\phi = (1 - \varepsilon)\tan\psi$, and a
nonlinear reparametrization does not preserve which Fourier orders
are present.

**First, the geometry-update algebra is derived in $\psi$.** This is
the load-bearing point and it is easy to miss. Consider a trial
ellipse displaced from the true isophote by a small offset $\delta$
along its major axis, and parametrize position on the trial ellipse
by the eccentric anomaly: $(a\cos\psi,\ b\sin\psi)$ in the ellipse
frame. The semi-major axis of the *isophote* passing through that
sample point is $a' \approx a - \delta\cos\psi$, so the sampled
intensity is
$I \approx I_0 - (dI/da)\,\delta\cos\psi$ — a pure $\cos\psi$ term.
Repeating the exercise for a minor-axis offset gives a pure
$\sin\psi$ term with an extra factor $(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}$; for an
ellipticity error, a pure $\cos 2\psi$ term with coefficient
$(dI/da)\,a\,\delta\varepsilon / [2(1-\varepsilon)]$; for a
position-angle error, a pure $\sin 2\psi$ term. Inverting those four
relations gives exactly the four update formulas ISOSTER applies —
including the $(1-\varepsilon)$, $2(1-\varepsilon)/a$ and
$[(1-\varepsilon)^2 - 1]$ factors that would otherwise look
arbitrary. In other words, the classical geometry corrections are
*already* an eccentric-anomaly result. Feeding them coefficients
measured in $\psi$ is exact; feeding them coefficients measured in
$\phi$ is an approximation whose error grows with ellipticity and
vanishes as $\varepsilon \to 0$, where $\phi \to \psi$.

**Second, orders mix under the reparametrization, and the mixing
runs downhill into the geometry terms.** Because the map between
the two angles is nonlinear, a residual that is a pure order-$n$
harmonic in one basis is *not* a pure order-$n$ harmonic in the
other. The direction that matters is the one that contaminates the
geometry update: genuine higher-order isophotal structure, expressed
in $\phi$, acquires first- and second-order components, and those
components are indistinguishable from a geometry mismatch. The
effect is easy to quantify. Take a residual that is exactly
$\cos 3\psi$ — pure third-order shape, no geometry error whatsoever
— on an isophote of axis ratio $b/a = 0.3$. Fitting the
five-parameter geometry model in $\psi$ returns all four
geometry coefficients as zero, correctly. Fitting the same data in
$\phi$ returns a first-order cosine coefficient of $-0.180$: an
$18\%$ spurious geometry signal conjured entirely out of real
third-order structure by the choice of angular coordinate. The
iteration then "corrects" a geometry error that does not exist.

Both problems are therefore about *interpretation* of the
coefficients rather than about the sampling being visibly sparse
somewhere, and both are absent for round isophotes and severe for
flattened ones — which is precisely the regime where edge-on disks
and peanut bulges live.

## §1.4.1.2 The eccentric-anomaly parametrization

The eccentric anomaly $\psi$ is the angular parameter that runs
uniformly along the ellipse when the latter is mapped to an
auxiliary unit circle. In the ellipse's principal coordinate system
(major axis along $x'$, minor along $y'$), the ellipse is the image
of the unit circle under the affine map
$(x', y') = (a\cos\psi, b\sin\psi)$. The arc-length increment per
unit $\psi$ is

$$
\frac{ds}{d\psi} = \sqrt{a^2 \sin^2\psi + b^2 \cos^2\psi},
$$

which varies between $b$ and $a$ over a full cycle, so its
maximum-to-minimum ratio is exactly $a / b$; it never vanishes, and
it is more uniform than $ds / d\phi$ at any fixed ellipticity. At
$b/a = 0.3$, for instance, $ds/d\psi$ spans a factor of $3.33$
against $4.87$ for $ds/d\phi$. The improvement is real but modest,
and it is not a clean power law: the $\phi$-density ratio has no
simple closed form and is *not* $(a/b)^2$, as an earlier version of
this section asserted. The sampling-uniformity argument is worth
making qualitatively and not worth over-quantifying, because — as
§1.4.1.1 sets out — it is the weaker of the two reasons to prefer
$\psi$ anyway.

The relationship between $\psi$ and the polar angle $\theta$
measured from the ellipse center (the true anomaly, not the image
position angle $\phi$ used elsewhere in this section) is

$$
\tan\theta = \frac{b}{a}\tan\psi = (1 - \varepsilon)\tan\psi.
$$

ISOSTER computes ellipse-boundary coordinates by stepping uniformly
through $\psi \in [0, 2\pi)$, evaluating $(x', y')$ on the principal
axes via the closed-form mapping, then rotating into image
coordinates by the position angle PA. The sampling step in
`isoster/sampling.py` is the same vectorized
`scipy.ndimage.map_coordinates` call described in §1.3.2; only the
coordinate construction differs. The harmonic fit is then performed
in the $\psi$-basis: the design matrix becomes

$$
\mathbf{A}_\psi = [\mathbf{1}, \sin\psi, \cos\psi, \sin 2\psi, \cos 2\psi,
\sin n_1\psi, \cos n_1\psi, \ldots],
$$

and the coefficients $A_n^\psi$, $B_n^\psi$ are the ones the
geometry-correction algebra of §1.4.1.1 was written for. For a
perfect ellipse the higher-order coefficients vanish in *either*
basis, as §1.4.1.1 established — that property is not what the
change of basis buys, and claiming it would contradict the argument
made there. What the $\psi$-basis buys is that a *non-zero* shape
deviation is expressed in the coordinate the correction formulas
assume, so it stays in the harmonic order it belongs to instead of
leaking down into orders 1 and 2.

One subtlety bears emphasizing, because it is easy to describe
incorrectly. The eccentric anomaly is defined relative to the
*current trial geometry* — its definition depends on the trial
$\varepsilon$ and PA — so as the iteration updates the geometry, the
mapping $\psi \to (x_\mathrm{image}, y_\mathrm{image})$ updates with
it. The natural worry is that feeding $\psi$-basis coefficients into
a geometry-update rule derived in the $\phi$-basis is a category
error, and that one should convert the coefficients back to
$\phi$-space first.

ISOSTER does not perform that conversion, and neither does ISOFIT.
The coefficients $(A_1, B_1, A_2, B_2)$ fitted in $\psi$ are fed
directly to the classical update formulas of §1.1, which map the
dominant amplitude onto $(x_0, y_0, \varepsilon, \mathrm{PA})$
through the local radial gradient. The polar angle $\phi$ is carried
alongside $\psi$ through sampling and sigma-clipping, but only so
that the angular bookkeeping stays aligned for diagnostics and error
propagation; it does not enter the update.

No conversion is needed, because — as §1.4.1.1 sets out — the
correction formulas were derived in $\psi$ in the first place. The
$\psi$-basis coefficients are exactly the quantities those formulas
expect, so consuming them directly is the *correct* operation, not a
tolerated approximation. It is the default $\phi$-mode that feeds
the formulas coefficients from the wrong basis, and does so for
compatibility with IRAF `Ellipse` and `photutils.isophote` rather
than because it is more accurate.

It is worth resisting a tempting but invalid defence of the
$\phi$-mode here, since it is the kind of argument that sounds
right. One might argue that the iteration is driven to a fixed point
where the residual first- and second-order content vanishes, so the
two bases must agree on *where* the mismatch is zero even if they
disagree about its size mid-iteration. They need not. A nonlinear
reparametrization does not preserve which orders vanish: the
$\cos 3\psi$ example of §1.4.1.1 has exactly zero first- and
second-order content in $\psi$ and a first-order coefficient of
$-0.180$ in $\phi$, so the two bases place the fixed point in
different locations. The fixed points coincide only in the limit
$\varepsilon \to 0$.

The practical consequences for a user are therefore narrower than
"the flag only changes the basis". The geometry parameters do retain
their classical geometric meaning, and the converged
$(x_0, y_0, \varepsilon, \mathrm{PA})$ remain directly comparable
between modes in the sense that they denote the same things. But the
two modes do not follow the same path, and on a flattened isophote
with real higher-order structure they need not converge to the same
place either. Which one to believe is not a matter of taste: the
$\psi$-mode geometry is the one whose corrections were derived for
the coefficients being fed to them.

## §1.4.1.3 ISOFIT-style joint harmonics

The default ISOSTER fit (and the classical Jedrzejewski / photutils
fit) restricts the inside-loop harmonic basis to orders 1 and 2:

$$
\mathbf{A}_5 = [\mathbf{1}, \sin\theta, \cos\theta, \sin 2\theta, \cos 2\theta],
$$

where $\theta$ is the angular basis ($\phi$ or $\psi$ depending on
the sampling mode). Higher-order coefficients $(A_n, B_n)$ for
$n \ge 3$ are fitted *post-hoc* on the converged geometry: each
order $n$ is fit separately with a three-column design matrix
$[\mathbf{1}, \sin n\theta, \cos n\theta]$ on the sampled profile
(the `compute_deviations` path). This post-hoc approach has two
advantages: the inside-loop solve is
cheap (the design matrix has only five columns); and the
convergence criterion is decoupled from how many higher orders the
user wants to measure.

It has one disadvantage. The higher-order coefficients describe
isophotal shape that is potentially correlated with the geometry
update: a slight error in the converged geometry can leak into the
higher-order coefficients through the cross-correlation of the
basis functions on the sampled intensity profile. For weak
deviations from ellipticity the leakage is negligible, and the
post-hoc approach is the right trade-off. For strong deviations —
X-shaped bulges, peanut bulges, strongly disky / boxy isophotes —
the leakage can be a significant fraction of the higher-order
amplitude itself, and the converged geometry can be biased away
from the geometry that would minimize the *full* harmonic residual
including the higher orders.

ISOFIT (Ciambur 2015) addresses this by performing the joint
inside-loop fit on an *extended* design matrix that includes the
higher-order columns:

$$
\mathbf{A}_\mathrm{ISOFIT} = [\mathbf{1}, \sin\theta, \cos\theta, \sin 2\theta, \cos 2\theta,
\sin n_1\theta, \cos n_1\theta, \ldots, \sin n_L\theta, \cos n_L\theta],
$$

where $\{n_1, \ldots, n_L\}$ are the orders specified in
`harmonic_orders` (default $\{3, 4\}$, with $L = 2$). The
linear-system solve is now $p = 5 + 2L$ parameters wide rather
than $5$, and the cross-correlations between geometry harmonics
and higher-order harmonics are absorbed into the joint covariance
matrix rather than leaked into the geometry update.

ISOSTER provides two ISOFIT variants, both gated by
`simultaneous_harmonics = True`:

- **`isofit_mode = 'in_loop'`** runs the joint solve on every
  iteration of every isophote. This is the most aggressive variant:
  the geometry update at each iteration is computed from the joint
  solve, so the geometry trajectory through iteration space differs
  from the post-hoc fit. The cross-correlations are absorbed
  throughout the iteration, not just at the end.

- **`isofit_mode = 'original'`** runs the classical 5-parameter
  fit inside the loop and, after convergence, fits all requested
  higher orders jointly in a single post-hoc solve on the
  converged geometry. The post-hoc design matrix is
  $[\mathbf{1}, \sin n_1\theta, \cos n_1\theta, \ldots]$ — it
  carries the higher-order columns *without* the geometry columns,
  so the orders share one solve and their cross-correlations are
  absorbed into the joint covariance, but the geometry harmonics
  are not re-fit. This matches the original Ciambur (2015)
  procedure's intent: the inside-loop trajectory is identical to
  the default, and only the reported higher-order coefficients
  (and their uncertainties) differ from the per-order post-hoc
  path. (The multi-band `'simultaneous_original'` mode of §1.4.5
  goes one step further and re-solves the *full* system — geometry
  columns included — once post-hoc; the two paths deliberately
  differ in that respect.)

In both variants, the extended linear system is solved through
the same NumPy / LAPACK pipeline used in the 5-parameter fit, and
the dominant-coefficient selector for the geometry update
continues to operate on indices 1–4 of the coefficient vector
(corresponding to $A_1, B_1, A_2, B_2$). Coefficient storage in
the output dictionary follows the convention
$[I_0, A_1, B_1, A_2, B_2, A_{n_1}, B_{n_1}, A_{n_2}, B_{n_2}, \ldots]$,
with the $k$-th higher order occupying indices $5 + 2k$ and $5 + 2k + 1$.

## §1.4.1.4 Caveats and fallbacks

Three caveats apply to the ISOFIT path and are worth flagging
explicitly:

**Sample-count fallback.** The extended design matrix requires at
least $2L + 5$ sample points to be well-posed. At small SMAs where
$N$ is at the floor of $\max(64, \lfloor 2\pi a \rfloor) = 64$, the
condition holds easily for $L = 2$ ($2L + 5 = 9$) but begins to
strain for $L = 4$ or larger when combined with severe masking
(say, more than 80% of the ellipse masked out by a defect or a
nearby source). When the available sample count falls below the
threshold, ISOSTER falls back to the 5-parameter fit for that
isophote and emits a `RuntimeWarning` on the first occurrence, so
the fallback is announced rather than silent.
The fallback is byte-equivalent to running with
`simultaneous_harmonics = False` for that isophote alone; the rest
of the fit proceeds with the joint solve.

**Compute overhead.** The two features cost very different amounts,
and quoting one figure for the pair obscures which one to worry
about. Measured on a whole-galaxy fit ($n = 2$ Sérsic,
$R_e = 30\,\mathrm{px}$, $\varepsilon = 0.4$, 59 isophotes,
`harmonic_orders=[3, 4]`) against the same fit in the default
configuration:

| configuration | wall time | ratio to default |
|---|---|---|
| default ($\phi$, post-hoc) | 48.9 ms | 1.00 |
| eccentric anomaly only | 51.0 ms | 1.04 |
| ISOFIT `in_loop` only | 62.2 ms | 1.27 |
| eccentric anomaly + ISOFIT | 63.8 ms | 1.30 |

(Absolute times are from one session and are machine- and
session-dependent; the ratios are the less machine-dependent part, though
not portable either — hardware, BLAS and library versions shift ratios too. Across eighteen
sessions the combined ratio has a median of $1.34$ and an
interquartile range of $1.32$–$1.36$. Reproduced by
`benchmarks/draft_timings/run_draft_timings.py`; measurement
protocol in §1.6.5.)

The eccentric anomaly is nearly free — it changes how the sample
coordinates are computed, not how much work is done. Essentially
all of the cost is ISOFIT's wider linear system, at about $30\%$
for $L = 2$ orders, and it will grow with the number of orders and
vary with the platform's LAPACK.
For most science use cases the overhead is dwarfed by the cost of
the image-level outward loop, but it is real and should be
budgeted when running ISOSTER on large samples.

**Geometry-update path unchanged.** Both the `'in_loop'` and the
`'original'` variant update the geometry through the same
coordinate-descent rule used by the default fit: the largest of
$|A_1|, |B_1|, |A_2|, |B_2|$ is identified, and the corresponding
geometry parameter is updated by an amount proportional to the
amplitude scaled by the local radial gradient. The ISOFIT path does
*not* introduce a multi-parameter update step; the geometry
trajectory through iteration space differs from the default only
because the harmonic coefficients themselves differ (their values
absorb cross-correlations with the higher orders). This is
intentional and matches Ciambur (2015) §4: the joint solve
clarifies the values, not the update rule.

A practical point worth mentioning: the combination
`use_eccentric_anomaly = True` *and* `simultaneous_harmonics = True`
with `isofit_mode = 'in_loop'` is the closest faithful
reimplementation of the Ciambur (2015) IRAF `Isofit` task within
the ISOSTER framework. It is the recommended configuration for
edge-on disks, peanut bulges, and strongly barred galaxies where
the higher-order harmonic structure carries the science of
interest, and where the user wants both the sampling-basis
correction and the cross-correlation absorption.

## §1.4.1.5 Demonstration: synthetic edge-on disk

To make the difference visible we use a synthetic edge-on disk
generated through the MockGal renderer in `isophote_test` (an
external companion repository, not part of the ISOSTER source
tree). The
recipe is a single-component Sersic profile with
$n_\mathrm{Sersic} = 1.0$ (exponential), effective semi-major axis
$R_e = 60\,\mathrm{px}$, ellipticity $\varepsilon = 0.70$
(near-edge-on aspect ratio $a / b \approx 3.3$), position angle
$\mathrm{PA} = 45^\circ$, and total flux scaled so the central
surface brightness is well above the sky noise. The image is
$512 \times 512\,\mathrm{px}$ with Gaussian sky noise corresponding
to $\mu_\mathrm{sky} = 26\,\mathrm{mag\,arcsec^{-2}}$ in a fiducial
band, and the rendered image is convolved with a Gaussian PSF of
FWHM $= 2\,\mathrm{px}$ before noise addition.

**A note on what this demonstration must not be.** An earlier
version of this subsection proposed a *pure* elliptical mock and
predicted that the $\phi$-mode fit would show a spurious rise in
$A_4^\mathrm{norm}$ that the $\psi$-mode fit would not. That
prediction is wrong, for the reason given in §1.4.1.1: a perfect
ellipse has constant intensity along its boundary, and a constant
has zero harmonic content in either angular coordinate. Both modes
would return $A_4^\mathrm{norm} \approx 0$ and the figure would show
nothing. A demonstration built on a pure ellipse cannot exhibit the
effect, because there is no effect to exhibit.

The mock therefore carries a *genuine* fourth-order deviation. We
add a disky perturbation to the exponential disk above, of
normalized amplitude $B_4^\mathrm{norm} = 0.03$ applied uniformly
across the SMA range, on top of the $\varepsilon = 0.70$ base
ellipse. The truth is then non-trivial and known: the correct
recovered profile is a flat line at $0.03$, and the correct geometry
is the planted $(\varepsilon, \mathrm{PA})$ at every radius.

**Which geometry parameter should be affected, and which should
not.** The perturbation is *aligned* with the ellipse axes, so it
enters as a $\cos 4\psi$ term. Re-expressed in $\phi$ at
$b/a = 0.3$, that term projects onto the five-parameter geometry
model with $B_2 = -0.538$ per unit amplitude and $A_2 = 0.000$ — it
contaminates the coefficient that drives *ellipticity* and, by
symmetry, leaves the position-angle coefficient untouched. (The
misaligned partner $\sin 4\psi$ does the reverse, projecting onto
$A_2$ with the same magnitude.) An earlier version of this
subsection predicted a bias in both $\varepsilon$ and PA from an
aligned perturbation; that was wrong, and the symmetry makes the
prediction sharp rather than vague: for this mock the $\phi$-mode
ellipticity should be biased and the $\phi$-mode PA should not.
A figure showing a PA bias here would indicate a bug, not the
effect.

**The comparison must vary one thing at a time.** Contrasting a
default $\phi$-mode post-hoc fit against an EA+ISOFIT joint fit
changes the angular basis *and* the harmonic-fit strategy at once,
so any difference is unattributable. The demonstration is therefore
a $2 \times 2$: $\{\phi, \psi\} \times \{$post-hoc, ISOFIT
in-loop$\}$, four fits on the same image, mask, starting geometry,
`astep` and `maxsma`. The basis effect is then the difference along
one axis at fixed strategy, and the ISOFIT effect the difference
along the other.

Panel C is the load-bearing panel, and it is the one the earlier
draft omitted: the argument for the eccentric anomaly is about the
*geometry* being contaminated by real higher-order structure, not
about a phantom higher-order signal appearing on a featureless
ellipse.

The recipe for the $2 \times 2$ is:

```python
from itertools import product
from isoster import fit_image
from isoster.config import IsosterConfig

common = dict(sma0=10.0, maxsma=180.0, harmonic_orders=[3, 4],
              x0=256.0, y0=256.0, eps=0.5, pa=0.0)

results = {}
for basis, strategy in product(("phi", "psi"), ("posthoc", "isofit")):
    cfg = IsosterConfig(
        **common,
        use_eccentric_anomaly=(basis == "psi"),
        simultaneous_harmonics=(strategy == "isofit"),
        isofit_mode="in_loop" if strategy == "isofit" else "original",
        compute_deviations=True,
    )
    results[(basis, strategy)] = fit_image(image, mask=mask, config=cfg)
```

All four use the same starting geometry
($x_0 = y_0 = 256\,\mathrm{px}$, $\varepsilon = 0.5$,
$\mathrm{PA} = 0$), so the two axes of the comparison are cleanly
separated. The output dictionaries can be passed to
`isoster.plotting.plot_comparison_qa_figure` to build the panels.
These runs have not been performed; the block above is the intended
recipe, not a record of an executed experiment.

A final note on when *not* to enable this combination. On
round-ish isophotes ($\varepsilon \lesssim 0.2$) both effects of
§1.4.1.1 are small — $\phi \to \psi$, so the correction formulas are
being fed nearly the right coefficients and the order-mixing is
weak — and the post-hoc higher-harmonic fit is consistent with the
joint solve. Switching to EA+ISOFIT in that regime adds the ~30%
compute overhead measured above without producing scientifically
distinguishable results. The $\varepsilon \lesssim 0.2$ boundary is a
rule of thumb from the scaling of §1.4.1.1 — both effects fall away
as $\phi \to \psi$ — and not a measured threshold; where exactly it
sits depends on how much genuine higher-order structure the galaxy
carries, since that is what leaks into the geometry terms. The
combination is therefore reserved for the regime where the user has
identified strong elliptical or non-elliptical isophote structure as
a science target; on a survey pipeline the simplest
policy is to apply EA+ISOFIT only to galaxies with morphological
classifications or photometric proxies indicating high-ellipticity
or peanut-bulge morphology.
