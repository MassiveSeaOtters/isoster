# §1.4.3 Low-surface-brightness outskirt strategy

The fitting of galaxy isophotes in the low-surface-brightness (LSB)
regime — at radii where the galaxy intensity has dropped to within a
factor of a few of the sky noise — is a different problem from the
fitting of inner isophotes, even though the algorithm is nominally
unchanged. Three things go wrong simultaneously. The radial intensity
gradient $dI/da$ shrinks toward the photon-noise floor, so the
geometry-update step (which divides each harmonic amplitude by
$dI/da$) becomes unstable: small numerator noise produces large
denominator-amplified geometry corrections. The harmonic-fit
residual RMS drops below the sky noise per sample, so the convergence
criterion $|a_\max| < c \cdot \sigma_\mathrm{eff}$ would let the
solver chase noise-induced asymmetries indefinitely if not for the
$\sigma_\mathrm{bg}/\sqrt{N}$ floor of §1.1. And contaminants —
faint companions, scattered light, diffuse intracluster light, a
bright source's extended PSF wings — that were negligible in the
inner high-$S/N$ region now contribute fractional intensity
perturbations of order unity to the isophote, biasing the
geometry-update direction.

The textbook solution is to fix the geometry — center, ellipticity,
position angle — at some user-chosen SMA and let the outer isophotes
be evaluated as forced photometry on that fixed geometry. This works
but is harsh in two ways. The transition SMA is set by hand, which
is a per-galaxy parameter that scales poorly to survey samples; and
the transition is binary, with no graceful degradation between the
fully-free inner fit and the fully-fixed outer extraction. ISOSTER
introduces two complementary features that together replace the
hand-tuned transition with an adaptive one. *Outer-region center
regularization* is a soft mechanism: a logistic Tikhonov ramp that
damps geometry updates in the outer SMA range toward an inner
reference centroid, with strength that grows continuously from
negligible (at $a = a_0$) to substantial (at $a \gg a_0$). It runs
in every outward iteration. The *automatic LSB geometry lock* is a
hard mechanism: a one-way state-machine triggered by per-isophote
gradient diagnostics that, once committed, freezes the geometry to
the last clean isophote and runs the remaining outward growth as
forced extraction with a robust integrator. The two features are
independent — either can be used alone — but they are designed to
compose: the soft regularization runs in the pre-lock phase and is
silently absorbed into the locked clone when the hard lock fires.

This section explains both features and their composition. We
focus on the algorithmic and implementation details; the empirical
performance of the strategy across many galaxies belongs to the
benchmark chapters.

## §1.4.3.1 Soft outer-region center regularization

### Motivation and design

The classical algorithm has no information about where the *real*
galaxy center is, beyond what the harmonic fit at each isophote
tells it. In the inner high-$S/N$ region this is sufficient: every
isophote converges to nearly the same $(x_0, y_0)$, and the
geometry is well-constrained. In the outer LSB region the same
harmonic fit on a much noisier signal produces a much larger
parameter covariance, and the per-isophote center estimate begins
to wander. The wandering is not random — contaminants pull the
center systematically toward themselves; PSF wings of a nearby
bright source bias the centroid along a fixed direction; an
incompletely-masked companion shifts the fitted center toward the
companion's position. The outward geometry trajectory can drift by
several pixels over the LSB tail and produce isophotes that no
longer share a center with the inner ones.

The soft regularization assumes that the inner isophotes, where the
center is well-determined, carry the *correct* center information,
and that the outer fit should be encouraged to remain consistent
with it. The encouragement is implemented as a Tikhonov-style
penalty on the geometry-update step: each step of size $\Delta g$
in a geometry coordinate is multiplied by a shrinkage factor that
grows with both SMA and the magnitude of the proposed step. Steps
that are small (the geometry is moving slowly anyway) are barely
affected; steps that are large (the geometry is about to make a big
excursion) are damped most strongly. The mechanism has no effect on
the inner isophotes, no effect on small geometry updates anywhere,
and the largest effect on the very updates that the user is most
concerned about — large excursions in the LSB tail.

### The logistic ramp and the inner reference

Two ingredients enter the soft regularization. The first is a
*scalar strength* $\lambda(a)$ that grows continuously with SMA:

$$
\lambda(a) = \frac{\lambda_0}{1 + \exp\!\left(-\dfrac{a - a_\mathrm{onset}}{\Delta a_\mathrm{ramp}}\right)},
$$

where $\lambda_0$ is the asymptotic strength (config field
`outer_reg_strength`, default $0$, suggested LSB value 1 to 3),
$a_\mathrm{onset}$ is the SMA at which the ramp reaches half its
asymptotic value (config `outer_reg_sma_onset`), and
$\Delta a_\mathrm{ramp}$ controls the width of the transition
(config `outer_reg_sma_width`). The logistic shape was chosen over
a step function and a linear ramp for two reasons: it is smooth
(no derivative discontinuities at the transition), and it has
explicit "on" and "off" tails (the ramp asymptotes to $0$ for
$a \ll a_\mathrm{onset}$ and to $\lambda_0$ for $a \gg
a_\mathrm{onset}$), which makes the strength interpretable at the
endpoints. The width $\Delta a_\mathrm{ramp}$ is naturally chosen
to be a small multiple of the SMA step `astep`; the default
recommendation is $\Delta a_\mathrm{ramp} = 0.1 \cdot a_\mathrm{max}$,
which keeps the ramp gentle on a typical SMA grid.

The second ingredient is the *inner reference geometry*. ISOSTER
constructs this once, between the inward loop and the outward
loop. Let $\mathcal{I}_\mathrm{inner}$ be the set of inward
isophotes plus the anchor isophote at $a_0$ that satisfy two
conditions: stop code $\in \{0, 1, 2\}$ (acceptable for
propagation) and $a \le a_0 \cdot f_\mathrm{ref}$ where
$f_\mathrm{ref}$ is the config field
`outer_reg_ref_sma_factor` (default $2.0$). The reference is the
flux-weighted mean of their geometries:

$$
g_\mathrm{ref}
= \frac{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}_\mathrm{inner}} w_i \, g_i}
       {\sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}_\mathrm{inner}} w_i},
\qquad
w_i = I_{0, i},
$$

where $g$ stands separately for each of the four geometry
coordinates $(x_0, y_0, \varepsilon, \mathrm{PA})$ and $I_{0,i}$ is
the inner-isophote intensity. The flux weighting concentrates the
reference on the high-$S/N$ isophotes nearest the galaxy center,
where the geometry is best determined.

If $\mathcal{I}_\mathrm{inner}$ is empty — typically because the
inward loop never converged or because no inner isophotes met the
stop-code cut — the reference falls back to the anchor isophote
geometry at $a_0$ alone. A `UserWarning` is emitted so the caller
knows the regularization is operating on a single reference point
rather than a flux-weighted average.

### Per-axis weights and the per-coordinate shrinkage factor

Once $\lambda(a)$ and $g_\mathrm{ref}$ are in hand, the
regularization acts on each geometry coordinate independently. Let
$\Delta g$ be a proposed update to coordinate $g$ at SMA $a$ (one
of the four axes: $\Delta x_0$, $\Delta y_0$, $\Delta
\varepsilon$, $\Delta \mathrm{PA}$). The shrinkage factor is

$$
\alpha(g, a)
= \frac{\lambda(a) \, w_g \, (\Delta g)^2}{1 + \lambda(a) \, w_g \, (\Delta g)^2},
$$

with $w_g$ the per-axis weight from `outer_reg_weights` (default
$\{x_0\!: 1, y_0\!: 1, \varepsilon\!: 1, \mathrm{PA}\!: 1\}$; an
axis weight of zero disables the regularization on that axis
entirely). The shrunk update under `outer_reg_mode = 'damping'`
(default) is

$$
\Delta g_\mathrm{damped} = (1 - \alpha) \, \Delta g,
$$

which leaves the direction of the update unchanged but reduces its
magnitude. For small $\Delta g$ the shrinkage $\alpha \to 0$ and
the update is unaffected; for large $\Delta g$ in the LSB regime
(large $\lambda(a)$) the shrinkage $\alpha \to 1$ and the update
is essentially zeroed. The closed form has the convenient property
that $\alpha \in [0, 1)$ identically, so the damped update never
overshoots the original step direction.

Under `outer_reg_mode = 'solver'`, the regularization additionally
pulls the geometry toward the reference. The effective update
becomes

$$
\Delta g_\mathrm{solver} = (1 - \alpha) \, \Delta g + \alpha \,
(g_\mathrm{ref} - g),
$$

i.e. a convex combination of the proposed step and a step toward
the inner reference, weighted by the same shrinkage factor. The
`'solver'` mode is more aggressive in the sense that it actively
moves the geometry back toward the inner reference even when the
proposed update happens to be zero; the `'damping'` mode is purely
a shrinkage and leaves the equilibrium where the iteration would
otherwise have placed it. For most science cases the `'damping'`
mode suffices; the `'solver'` mode is reserved for the most
demanding contamination scenarios where the LSB tail is being
actively pulled away from the inner geometry.

### Selector-level penalty

A complementary mechanism operates one level higher. ISOSTER's
fit loop tracks not only the per-iteration converged geometry but
also a *best-iteration* candidate via the effective amplitude
$a_\mathrm{eff} = |a_\max| + \mathcal{R}(g)$ (§1.1). The
regularization contributes to $\mathcal{R}$ a penalty term

$$
\mathcal{R}_\mathrm{outer}(g, a) = \lambda(a) \sum_{g\text{-axes}} w_g \, (g - g_\mathrm{ref})^2,
$$

so that, when multiple iterations have produced candidate
geometries with similar $|a_\max|$, the iteration whose geometry
is closest to the inner reference is preferred. This is a
soft-tie-breaking effect; the iteration that minimizes the bare
harmonic amplitude usually also minimizes the effective amplitude,
because the harmonic-amplitude term dominates in the high-$S/N$
regime. In the LSB regime the two terms become comparable, and the
regularization penalty bounds the cumulative drift through the
best-iteration selector even when the per-iteration shrinkage
factor would by itself permit larger excursions.

## §1.4.3.2 Hard automatic LSB geometry lock

The soft regularization is a *gentle* mechanism that scales with
SMA and step size; it does not enforce a hard upper bound on how
far the geometry can drift. For deep enough LSB tails the soft
regularization is insufficient on its own, and the algorithm needs
a *hard* mechanism that freezes the geometry once the per-isophote
diagnostics indicate that the fit has crossed into a regime where
free fitting is no longer scientifically meaningful.

### Trigger detector and debouncing

The detector runs after each outward isophote has converged (or
failed to converge) and inspects three per-isophote quantities:
the radial gradient $\widehat{dI/da}$, the gradient uncertainty
$\sigma_{dI/da}$, and the stop code. The relative gradient error

$$
\rho = \frac{\sigma_{dI/da}}{\left|\widehat{dI/da}\right|}
$$

is the primary trigger. When $\rho$ exceeds a user-configurable
threshold $\rho_\mathrm{trig}$ (config field
`lsb_auto_lock_maxgerr`, default $0.30$) — or the isophote ended
with stop code $-1$ (a gradient-related failure) — the detector
flags the isophote as triggered. The threshold $\rho_\mathrm{trig}
= 0.30$ is deliberately tighter than the per-isophote gradient
acceptability threshold $\rho_\mathrm{accept} = 0.50$ (config
`maxgerr`) that produces stop code $-1$ on its own. This is the
key design choice: the lock detector fires *before* the gradient
checks would have terminated the outward growth, so the lock
commits while there are still clean isophotes upstream to anchor
to.

Single isolated triggers are not sufficient to commit the lock. A
faint companion intersected by a single isophote, an interpolation
artefact along one ellipse, or a transient noise spike can produce
a one-off elevated $\rho$ without indicating that the LSB
transition has actually been reached. The detector therefore
*debounces* the trigger: $N_\mathrm{deb}$ consecutive outward
isophotes (config field `lsb_auto_lock_debounce`, default $2$)
must all be triggered for the lock to commit. A clean isophote
appearing in the streak resets the counter to zero. The debounce
default of $2$ means that two consecutive bad isophotes are
required, which empirically distinguishes a genuine LSB transition
(where the gradient diagnostics break down monotonically as
$a$ grows) from isolated isophote failures.

### Anchor selection and the locked clone

When the debounce streak reaches $N_\mathrm{deb}$, the lock commits.
The locked-anchor isophote is *not* the most recent triggered
isophote (whose geometry may already have drifted); it is the
*last clean isophote before the streak began*. Concretely, if the
streak occupies indices $k, k+1, \ldots, k + N_\mathrm{deb} - 1$
in the outward growth list, the anchor is isophote $k - 1$. Its
geometry $(x_0^\star, y_0^\star, \varepsilon^\star,
\mathrm{PA}^\star)$ is the geometry that will be enforced on every
subsequent outward isophote.

The lock is implemented as a *cloned configuration*. ISOSTER calls

```python
locked_config = config.model_copy(update=dict(
    fix_center=True, fix_pa=True, fix_eps=True,
    integrator=config.lsb_auto_lock_integrator,
))
```

and the subsequent outward isophotes are fit with the locked
clone. Three concrete effects follow. First, the geometry-update
machinery (§1.1) sees `fix_center=True, fix_pa=True, fix_eps=True`
and zeros all four geometry coordinates' updates by zeroing the
corresponding harmonic amplitudes before the dominant-amplitude
selector runs. The fitted geometry is held at the anchor exactly;
no iterative drift is possible. Second, the integrator switches
from the default `'mean'` to whatever the user-configured
`lsb_auto_lock_integrator` is — by default `'median'`, which is
more robust to the asymmetric residual distributions typical of
LSB-regime contamination. Third, the lock is one-way: once
committed, the locked clone is used for the remainder of outward
growth and the original config is never restored within the same
`fit_image` call. The inward loop and the central pixel are
unaffected because both have already completed before the outward
loop begins.

The locked isophotes carry two additional output fields. The boolean
`lsb_locked` is set on every locked isophote so the caller can
filter the output by lock state. The boolean `lsb_auto_lock_anchor`
appears only on the first locked isophote (the one immediately
following the anchor), serving as a marker in QA overlays for the
exact SMA at which the lock committed. The top-level results dict
gains three companion fields: `lsb_auto_lock` (echo that the
feature was on), `lsb_auto_lock_sma` (the SMA at which the lock
committed, or `None` if the detector never triggered), and
`lsb_auto_lock_count` (the number of outward isophotes fit under
the locked clone).

### Implementation prerequisites and conflict checks

The detector reads `grad`, `grad_error`, and `grad_r_error` from
each outward isophote's output dictionary. These fields are only
present when `debug=True` is set in the config. ISOSTER's config
validator does *not* require the caller to set `debug=True`
explicitly; instead, when `lsb_auto_lock=True` is set with
`debug=False`, ISOSTER emits a `UserWarning` and internally clones
the config with `debug=True` for the duration of the fit. The
caller's original config remains unchanged. This trade is
deliberate: forcing the caller to enable a debug flag for a
production feature would be confusing, and the storage overhead of
the three extra fields per isophote is negligible.

The Pydantic validator enforces three hard rejection rules at
config-construction time. `lsb_auto_lock=True` cannot coexist with
`fix_center=True`, `fix_pa=True`, or `fix_eps=True`; the
combination is rejected with `ValidationError` because the auto-lock
is *itself* a mechanism for fixing the geometry, and pairing it
with manual fixed-geometry flags would be either redundant or
self-contradictory depending on which fix is meant to take
precedence. The auto-lock is also silently inactive when combined
with template-based forced photometry (`template=…`); a
`UserWarning` is emitted at the top of `fit_image` and the feature
is bypassed entirely. The reason is that template-based forced
extraction already enforces fixed geometry per SMA from the
template, leaving nothing for the lock to do.

## §1.4.3.3 Composition of the two mechanisms

Both features are *independent* in the sense that either can be
enabled without the other. They are also *designed to compose*:
when both are on, the soft regularization runs from $a_0$ outward
and gradually damps the geometry trajectory; if the LSB tail is
deep enough that the gradient diagnostics begin to break down, the
hard lock then commits and freezes the geometry for the remainder.

The composition is mechanically clean. The locked-clone config that
the auto-lock installs has `fix_center=True, fix_pa=True,
fix_eps=True`, which zeroes the geometry-update directions before
the soft regularization shrinkage would have a chance to multiply
them. The shrinkage factor $\alpha$ is multiplying a zero step, so
the regularization is a silent no-op for the locked tail. There is
no need to disable the regularization explicitly when the lock
fires; the math takes care of it.

The intended division of labor between the two mechanisms can be
read off this composition. The soft regularization is a *prophylactic
measure* that operates while the gradient diagnostics are still
within tolerance: it bounds the cumulative drift in the pre-LSB
regime, where the fit is technically still valid but where small
contamination can pull the centroid off its inner-isophote
consensus. The hard lock is a *terminal measure* that activates
when the gradient diagnostics indicate the fit can no longer be
trusted to follow the galaxy. The two failure modes the strategy is
designed to address are therefore different in character:
contamination-driven drift in the pre-lock regime, and noise-driven
divergence in the post-lock regime. Each mechanism is targeted at
one of them, and the composition produces a single ISOSTER fit that
spans both regimes without a hand-tuned transition.

A practical observation. The auto-lock's debounce default of $2$
and the trigger threshold of $0.30$ are calibrated so that the
lock typically commits a few isophotes before the unconstrained
fit would have produced stop code $-1$. The soft regularization
therefore operates over a non-zero range of SMAs before the lock
fires, and that range can be substantial — tens of SMAs on a
deeply-fit galaxy. The soft regularization is not a vestigial
warm-up step; it is doing real work in that range.

## §1.4.3.4 Caveats

The two features carry a small number of caveats that bear on
when to enable them.

**Both features are disabled by default.** The user has to opt in
explicitly. ISOSTER's default fit is the classical Jedrzejewski /
photutils behaviour with no LSB-regime intervention. This is the
right default for fits that do not push into LSB territory; the
features should be enabled when the user is fitting deep imaging
where they expect contamination or noise-driven drift.

**The soft regularization is still being calibrated across surveys.**
The default parameter values
(`outer_reg_sma_onset`, `outer_reg_sma_width`, `outer_reg_strength`)
are heuristic recommendations; the values that work well on HSC
edge cases may be sub-optimal on DECaLS, which has different
seeing, sky brightness, and noise characteristics. The
recommended-range values in `docs/02-configuration-reference.md`
are starting points, not survey-blind defaults.

**The hard lock detector reads gradient diagnostics, not the image
directly.** It is therefore agnostic to the image content beyond
what the per-isophote fit reports. A galaxy with structurally
unusual outskirts (truncated disks, tidal streams) may produce
gradient diagnostics that look like LSB-regime divergence even
when the image data are still informative. The lock will commit
in that case, and the user will need to inspect the locked tail
to decide whether the freeze was appropriate. The output fields
`lsb_auto_lock_sma` and `lsb_locked` make this inspection
straightforward.

**The lock is one-way per fit.** There is no "unlock and re-fit
free" mode. If the lock commits early on a galaxy where the user
believed the inner region warranted a longer free fit, the
recommended workflow is to raise the trigger threshold
`lsb_auto_lock_maxgerr` (or the debounce count
`lsb_auto_lock_debounce`) and re-run, rather than to post-process
the locked isophotes back into free isophotes. The locked
isophotes are *output* with their fixed-geometry origin clearly
marked; they should be interpreted as forced photometry, not as
free-fit results.

**Forced photometry through `template=…` disables both features
silently.** This is a property of the template-based forced
extraction path, which is structurally distinct from the regular
iterative driver. A `UserWarning` is emitted at the top of
`fit_image` to make the bypass visible. Users who need the LSB
strategy on a multi-band workflow should prefer the joint
multi-band free fit of §1.4.5, which does pass the LSB features
through to each band's converged geometry.

## §1.4.3.5 Demonstration: deep mock with bright contaminant

To make the composition of the two features visible in isolation
we use a synthetic galaxy generated through the MockGal renderer.
The recipe is a single-component Sersic profile with
$n_\mathrm{Sersic} = 4.0$ (de Vaucouleurs-like), effective
semi-major axis $R_e = 50\,\mathrm{px}$, ellipticity $\varepsilon =
0.30$, and total flux scaled to deliver a central surface
brightness of $\mu_0 \approx 17.5\,\mathrm{mag\,arcsec^{-2}}$ in a
fiducial band. The image is $1024 \times 1024\,\mathrm{px}$ with
Gaussian sky noise corresponding to $\mu_\mathrm{sky} =
27.5\,\mathrm{mag\,arcsec^{-2}}$, deep enough that the outward fit
can be pushed to $a_\mathrm{max} = 400\,\mathrm{px}$ before the
gradient diagnostics start to fail. A *bright companion* — a
point source at $r \approx 220\,\mathrm{px}$ from the target's
center, with flux roughly $10\%$ of the target's central peak — is
added to the rendered image after PSF convolution but before noise.
The companion is masked out only in a small region around its
nominal centroid; its PSF wings extend across a substantial range
of outer SMAs and would, in an unconstrained fit, bias the centroid
toward the companion's direction.

The figure is constructed so that all three fits use the same
starting geometry, the same `astep`, the same `maxsma`, and the
same OLS-vs-WLS setting (we use OLS for clarity, but the qualitative
picture is identical under WLS); the only difference between the
three runs is the LSB-feature configuration. The argument the figure
makes is therefore narrow and concrete: each of the three runs is
an instance of the *same algorithm* with different LSB-regime
intervention, and the drift comparison isolates the effect of each
intervention. The free fit demonstrates the contamination failure
mode the strategy is designed to address. The soft-mode trajectory
demonstrates that the prophylactic measure bounds the drift even
without a hard transition. The combined soft+hard trajectory
demonstrates that the composition produces a single ISOSTER fit
spanning both the pre-lock and post-lock regimes without a
hand-tuned transition SMA.

The reproducibility recipe is:

```python
from isoster import fit_image
from isoster.config import IsosterConfig

# Free fit (baseline)
config_free = IsosterConfig(
    sma0=10.0, maxsma=400.0,
)

# Soft regularization only
config_soft = IsosterConfig(
    sma0=10.0, maxsma=400.0,
    use_outer_center_regularization=True,
    outer_reg_sma_onset=180.0,
    outer_reg_sma_width=20.0,
    outer_reg_strength=2.0,
    outer_reg_weights={"center": 1.0, "eps": 1.0, "pa": 1.0},
)

# Soft + hard, the recommended deep-LSB configuration
config_combined = IsosterConfig(
    sma0=10.0, maxsma=400.0,
    use_outer_center_regularization=True,
    outer_reg_sma_onset=180.0,
    outer_reg_sma_width=20.0,
    outer_reg_strength=2.0,
    outer_reg_weights={"center": 1.0, "eps": 1.0, "pa": 1.0},
    lsb_auto_lock=True,
    lsb_auto_lock_maxgerr=0.30,
    lsb_auto_lock_debounce=2,
    lsb_auto_lock_integrator="median",
    debug=True,
)

results_free     = fit_image(image, mask=mask, config=config_free)
results_soft     = fit_image(image, mask=mask, config=config_soft)
results_combined = fit_image(image, mask=mask, config=config_combined)

print("lock committed at sma =", results_combined["lsb_auto_lock_sma"])
print("locked isophote count =", results_combined["lsb_auto_lock_count"])
```

For the demo above, the `outer_reg_sma_onset = 180` was chosen to
place the soft-regularization ramp midpoint at the SMA where the
companion's PSF wings begin to contribute appreciably to the
isophote, so the soft mechanism is engaged precisely where the
contamination problem starts. The auto-lock then committed at an
SMA somewhat further out, demonstrating the soft-pre-lock /
hard-post-lock division of labor.

A closing observation. The LSB strategy is opt-in by design. On
shallow surveys, on bright nearby galaxies fit only to a few
effective radii, or on isolated targets with clean fields, the
classical free fit is the right answer and the LSB features add
nothing. The strategy is for the regime where the science case
explicitly requires pushing into the LSB outskirts and where
contamination or noise-driven divergence is anticipated: deep
imaging surveys (HSC-SSP, the eventual LSST main survey), nearby
galaxies in crowded fields, intracluster light measurements,
truncation-radius studies. In those regimes the strategy
quantifiably bounds the drift; in others it is a no-op, which is
also the right behaviour.
