# §1.4.2 Variance-aware fitting

The Jedrzejewski (1987) algorithm and the Python predecessors that
implement it fit harmonics by ordinary least squares with uniform
weights. Modern wide-field surveys — HSC-SSP, DECaLS / BASS / MzLS,
DES, eventually LSST — deliver a per-pixel inverse-variance map
alongside the science image, but the classical algorithm has no
mechanism for consuming it. The information is therefore thrown
away, with two consequences: outlier pixels (cosmic rays, hot
pixels, masked-bad pixels that survived the bad-pixel map) carry
the same weight in the harmonic fit as good pixels; and parameter
uncertainties are estimated from the *scatter on the ellipse* —
which conflates per-pixel photon noise with the intrinsic
azimuthal scatter from galaxy structure (spiral arms, dust lanes,
bars, faint companions) — rather than from the photon noise the
variance map already encodes.

ISOSTER closes this gap by accepting a `variance_map` argument to
`fit_image` and routing all harmonic fits through a weighted
least-squares (WLS) path when one is supplied. The path is
algorithmically separate from the OLS path: when
`variance_map = None` the linear-system solver is a plain classical
OLS solve and the output is bit-for-bit identical to
a `variance_map`-less call. When a variance map is supplied, every
harmonic fit on every isophote — geometry fit, ISOFIT extended fit,
post-hoc higher-order fit, forced-extraction fit, multi-band joint
fit — switches to WLS uniformly. There is no half-WLS configuration
in which some harmonic fits use weights and others do not.

## §1.4.2.1 OLS vs WLS at the algorithm level

The OLS normal equations for the design matrix $\mathbf{A}$ and the
sampled intensities $\mathbf{y}$ are

$$
\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{y},
$$

with parameter covariance

$$
\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})_\mathrm{OLS}
= (\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\,\hat\sigma_\mathrm{res}^2,
$$

where $\hat\sigma_\mathrm{res}^2$ is the *residual variance*
estimated empirically from the fit residuals,
$\hat\sigma_\mathrm{res}^2 = \mathrm{rms}(\mathbf{y} -
\mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{x}})^2$. The residual-variance rescaling is
necessary precisely because OLS has no external scale for the noise
— the matrix $(\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{A})^{-1}$ alone has the
wrong units, and the only available scale is what the residuals
themselves provide.

The WLS normal equations replace $\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{A}$
and $\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{y}$ with their weighted analogues,
using the diagonal weight matrix $\mathbf{W} = \mathrm{diag}(1 /
\sigma_i^2)$ where $\sigma_i^2$ is the per-pixel variance at the
$i$-th sample point along the ellipse:

$$
\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}
= \mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{y},
$$

with parameter covariance

$$
\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})_\mathrm{WLS}
= (\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A})^{-1}.
$$

The WLS covariance is *exact* in the sense that no residual-variance
rescaling is required. The inverse-variance weights have units of
$1/\mathrm{intensity}^2$, so the dimensional argument that forced
the OLS rescaling does not apply here:
$(\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A})^{-1}$ already has units
of $\mathrm{intensity}^2$ on the parameter axes that are themselves
in intensity units. The exactness is conditional on the variance
map being a correct description of the per-pixel photon noise;
when it is not (correlated noise from coaddition, underestimated
sky variance, calibration error in the photometric zeropoint), WLS
inherits the bias and produces over-confident error bars. We
return to this in §1.4.2.4.

The two practical consequences of switching from OLS to WLS are
worth naming explicitly. *Outlier down-weighting*: a single
hot-pixel sample with $\sigma_i^2 = 10^{6}$ relative to the
$\sigma^2 \sim 1$ of the neighbouring sky-noise pixels carries a
weight $10^{-6}$ times smaller than its neighbours in the harmonic
fit. The classical OLS path would weight that pixel equally and let
a single outlier swing the dominant harmonic amplitude;
WLS effectively masks it without the caller having to identify the
pixel ahead of time. *Error-bar decoupling*: in the inner
high-signal-to-noise region of a galaxy the OLS error bars are
dominated by genuine azimuthal scatter from spiral arms, dust
lanes, and bars, while the per-pixel photon noise is small; OLS and
WLS therefore agree on the error bar to within a constant factor
that depends on how much the intra-arm intensity varies. In the
LSB outskirts the relationship inverts: per-pixel photon noise is
comparable to or larger than the intrinsic intensity variation, the
OLS residual-variance estimate undershoots the true photon noise
because it averages over only $N$ sample points, and WLS error
bars are typically a factor of 1.5 to 2 larger than OLS — exactly
because they now reflect the photon noise the user already had in
hand.

## §1.4.2.2 Per-pixel gradient error propagation

The error bar on the mean intensity $I_0$ propagates
straightforwardly from the variance map. In the free fit, $I_0$ is
the intercept of the harmonic fit, so its variance is the
intercept element of the WLS parameter covariance,

$$
\mathrm{Var}(I_0)_\mathrm{WLS}
= \left[(\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\right]_{0,0},
$$

and ISOSTER reports $\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(I_0)}$ as `intens_err` in
the WLS path. (When the fit covariance is unavailable it falls back
to the variance of an inverse-variance-weighted mean,
$1 / \sum_i \sigma_i^{-2}$; template-based forced photometry, where
the reported intensity *is* a plain weighted mean, uses that exact
weighted-mean variance.) The OLS
analogue is $\mathrm{rms} / \sqrt{N}$, which inherits the
photon-noise-vs-azimuthal-scatter conflation discussed above.

The more interesting propagation is the *radial gradient* error,
because that is the quantity that drives the convergence criterion
and the gradient-SNR damping (§1.1, §1.3) and because its OLS
estimate is structurally biased in a way the intensity-error
estimate is not. The radial gradient $dI / da$ is computed in
ISOSTER from two samples at offset SMAs: a central sample at the
current $a$ and an offset sample at $a + \Delta a$ (or
$a (1 + \Delta a)$ for multiplicative SMA stepping). The two
samples produce two mean intensities $\bar I_c$ and $\bar I_g$
(plain unweighted means, or medians under the `'median'`
integrator), and the gradient is

$$
\widehat{dI/da}
= \frac{\bar I_g - \bar I_c}{\Delta r},
$$

with $\Delta r$ equal to the radial separation between the two
samples (either $\Delta a$ in linear-stepping mode or $a \cdot
\Delta a$ in multiplicative-stepping mode). Assuming the two
samples are statistically independent — they sample different
elliptical paths through the image and the per-pixel photon noise
is uncorrelated between distinct pixels — the variance of the
gradient is

$$
\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\widehat{dI/da}\right)
= \frac{\mathrm{Var}(\bar I_c) + \mathrm{Var}(\bar I_g)}{(\Delta r)^2},
$$

and the WLS gradient error is

$$
\sigma_{dI/da}^\mathrm{WLS}
= \frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\bar I_c) + \mathrm{Var}(\bar I_g)}}{\Delta r}
= \frac{\sqrt{\dfrac{\sum_i \sigma_{i,c}^2}{N_c^2}
         + \dfrac{\sum_j \sigma_{j,g}^2}{N_g^2}}}{\Delta r}.
$$

This is the formula ISOSTER uses internally when a variance map is
present and `integrator='mean'`: the per-ring means are unweighted,
and each per-mean variance is the ordinary variance of an
*unweighted* mean of independent, non-identically-distributed
measurements, $\mathrm{Var}(\bar I) = \sum_i \sigma_i^2 / N^2$ —
**not** the smaller variance of an inverse-variance-*weighted* mean,
$1 / \sum_i \sigma_i^{-2}$, that the reported *intensity* uses under
WLS (§1.4.2.1 above). Under `integrator='median'`, both rings use the
median instead, with the matching normal-theory variance
$\pi N / \left(2 \left(\sum_i \sigma_i^{-1}\right)^2\right)$, which
reduces to $\pi \sigma^2 / (2N)$ for uniform per-sample variance — the
ordinary mean-of-$N$ variance inflated by the median's
$\pi/2 \approx 1.571$ inefficiency factor. This median form is a
normal-theory approximation: a real ring carries azimuthal galaxy
structure and neighbouring samples are correlated through bilinear
interpolation, neither of which the derivation assumes, though Monte
Carlo tests against independent Gaussian samples agree with it to
within a few percent.

Matching the gradient's variance formula to the *same* estimator the
gradient itself uses — rather than reusing the weighted-mean formula
that the intensity uses — is deliberate: the intensity is a
per-isophote photometric measurement, where down-weighting noisy
pixels is desirable, while the gradient is a diagnostic comparison
between two rings, where matching the same, simple estimator on both
sides keeps the comparison honest. Using the weighted-mean variance
for an unweighted mean would *understate* the gradient's true
uncertainty whenever the per-pixel variances differ across the ring —
an earlier version of ISOSTER did exactly this; see
`docs/04-architecture.md`, section "Gradient error and ring
statistics", for the fixed formula and its measured consequences,
including a case where the corrected (larger) error changes which of
two candidate baseline SMAs the reported gradient comes from.

The expression is still *pure photon noise*: the only quantities
on the right-hand side are the per-pixel variances at the two
sampled ellipses, and the geometric separation $\Delta r$.
Galaxy-structure scatter does not appear, because the harmonic-fit
residuals are not used.

The OLS analogue, by contrast, replaces both $\mathrm{Var}(\bar
I_c)$ and $\mathrm{Var}(\bar I_g)$ with scatter-based estimates of
the form $s_c^2 / N_c$ and $s_g^2 / N_g$, where $s_c^2$ and $s_g^2$
are the sample-residual variances at the two ellipses. This
substitution looks innocent — both quantities have units of
$\mathrm{intensity}^2$ — but it conflates two different sources of
variance. The scatter $s_c^2$ at a single ellipse contains both the
per-pixel photon noise *and* the intrinsic azimuthal variation of
the galaxy structure at that radius. The latter is precisely what
the harmonic decomposition is trying to *measure*; subtracting its
fitted contribution would help, but the residual $s_c^2$ after the
five-parameter harmonic fit still carries azimuthal structure from
orders $n \ge 3$ that the geometry fit does not absorb. The OLS
gradient error is therefore systematically biased — usually
upward, sometimes downward depending on whether the geometry fit
has overshot the higher-order structure — and the bias depends on
the morphological complexity of the galaxy in a way that is
difficult to characterize a priori.

The bias matters because $\sigma_{dI/da}$ feeds the convergence
criterion through the gradient-SNR damping (§1.3.2): if the
gradient SNR is below 3, the geometry-update damping factor is
reduced dynamically to prevent overfitting the noise floor.
Overestimating $\sigma_{dI/da}$ — which is the typical OLS failure
mode on a galaxy with prominent spiral structure — causes ISOSTER
to damp the geometry update *more aggressively than the data
warrants*, slowing convergence. Underestimating it — typical for a
clean elliptical galaxy where the harmonic fit absorbs almost all
azimuthal variation — produces under-damped iterations that
oscillate around the converged geometry. WLS removes both biases
by replacing the scatter estimate with the variance-map propagation,
and the gradient-SNR criterion then reflects the actual photon noise.

A practical observation. The WLS gradient error formula above is
algebraically equivalent to the standard photon-noise propagation
for the difference of two means; nothing in it is novel. What is
novel is that the classical isophote-fit literature *predates* the
routine availability of per-pixel inverse-variance maps, and
implementations written in that era reasonably estimated gradient
errors from residual scatter because that was the only available
estimate. The WLS path in ISOSTER simply uses the better estimate
when the user supplies the data for it.

## §1.4.2.3 Input handling and safeguards

The `variance_map` argument is an array of the same shape as the
science image (a `ValueError` is raised at the top of `fit_image`
otherwise) carrying the per-pixel variance $\sigma_i^2$. The
following sanitization rules are applied in order:

- **Shape and dtype check.** The variance map is coerced to a
  float-64 NumPy array; integer or float-32 inputs are converted
  with a copy. The caller's array is never mutated.

- **NaN and infinite values.** A non-finite entry carries no usable
  information about the pixel's noise. ISOSTER marks it invalid and
  the sample at that pixel is dropped during sampling — excluded
  from both the ring statistic and its uncertainty, the same
  treatment already given to masked pixels — rather than being kept
  in the fit with a substitute value. A `RuntimeWarning` naming the
  affected pixel count is emitted once per `fit_image` call. NaN
  entries typically appear in survey inverse-variance maps at
  pixels masked out of the calibration; dropping them removes those
  pixels from the fit without the caller having to pre-process the
  map.

- **Non-positive values.** A variance of zero or negative is not
  physical and is likewise invalid: the corresponding sample is
  marked and dropped the same way, with its own `RuntimeWarning`.
  This is a deliberate change from an earlier version of ISOSTER,
  which instead clamped non-positive entries to $10^{-30}$ — an
  enormous but finite weight — on the reasoning that `invvar = 0`
  in a survey inverse-variance map denotes a pixel masked out of
  the calibration rather than a pixel with truly zero variance.
  That clamp kept the flagged pixel *inside* the ring statistic
  while giving it outsized influence: because a ring gradient's
  uncertainty combines the variances of two rings, a single
  clamped entry could shrink the reported gradient error by
  roughly fifteen orders of magnitude, silently defeating the
  `maxgerr` gradient-quality check, the signal-to-noise damping of
  the geometry update, and the low-surface-brightness auto-lock.
  Dropping the sample instead keeps the ring statistic and its
  uncertainty describing the identical set of pixels in every case.
  See `docs/04-architecture.md`, section "Invalid-variance policy",
  for the full account and a measured real-data example.

- **Copy-on-write.** The internal sanitized array is held in a
  buffer separate from the caller's `variance_map`. A caller can
  pass the same `variance_map` to multiple `fit_image` calls
  (typical of survey pipelines that fit the same galaxy at
  multiple bands or with multiple `IsosterConfig` variants)
  without worrying about cumulative side effects.

The canonical inverse-variance-to-variance conversion is:

```python
variance_map = np.where(invvar > 0, 1.0 / invvar, np.nan)
```

Writing this conversion explicitly is not required for
correctness — an unconverted `invvar = 0` pixel already produces
`variance = inf`, which ISOSTER marks invalid and drops during
sampling — but it remains good practice: it states, in the caller's
own code, which pixels are being excluded and why, rather than
relying on the reader knowing ISOSTER's internal validity rule.
Passing the same pixels through the `masks` argument instead is an
equally explicit alternative.

**Do not replace the `np.nan` in this recipe with a large finite
number such as `1e30`.** A literal `1e30` is finite and strictly
positive, so it passes the validity check described above and is
*kept* in the ring rather than dropped. Because `compute_gradient`'s
mean-error formula is $\mathrm{Var}(\bar{I}) = \Sigma_i v_i / N^2$,
a handful of `1e30` entries among a few hundred samples inflates the
reported `grad_error` by many orders of magnitude with no warning —
the opposite of the intended down-weighting. This can silently
defeat the `maxgerr` gradient-quality gate, the signal-to-noise
damping of the geometry update, and the low-surface-brightness
auto-lock. Use `np.nan` or `np.inf` for excluded pixels, or exclude
them through the `masks` argument instead.

## §1.4.2.4 Caveats and composability

Three caveats apply to the WLS path:

**Variance maps do not replace masking.** A pixel with $\sigma_i^2 =
10^{6}$ in the variance map still contributes a weight of $10^{-6}$
to the harmonic fit, not zero. For a single bad pixel on an
isophote with $N \sim 1000$ samples this is negligible; for
pathological cases where a large fraction of the ellipse is
covered by high-variance pixels (a defect that spans an arc of the
ellipse, a bright companion's diffraction spike crossing the
isophote) the residual weight can still bias the fit. The
recommended practice is to combine `variance_map` with a binary
`mask` array that excludes pixels the caller knows to be unusable;
WLS then operates on the remaining pixels with weights derived
from their physical photon noise.

**Variance maps inherit upstream systematics.** If the survey
inverse-variance map underestimates the true noise — typically the
case for coadded imaging where pixel-to-pixel noise is correlated
and the naive per-pixel variance does not reflect the effective
noise per resolution element — WLS produces over-confident error
bars on the harmonic-fit parameters and on the gradient. ISOSTER
cannot diagnose this from the variance map alone; the caller is
responsible for correcting the variance map upstream, typically
through a noise-rescaling factor estimated from blank-sky regions
or from a covariance-matrix model of the coaddition.

**High-S/N regime equivalence.** In the inner regions of a
well-resolved galaxy, where the per-pixel photon noise is much
smaller than the typical intra-isophote intensity variation, the
WLS weights are nearly uniform across the ellipse and the WLS
fit converges to the OLS fit. The error bars differ — WLS uses the
variance-map propagation, OLS the residual scatter — but the
fitted geometry, the harmonic coefficients, and the curve-of-growth
agree to within the LAPACK floating-point determinism. Users who
do not have a variance map should not invent one to enable WLS
"just in case"; the OLS path produces correct geometry estimates
on its own, and a fabricated variance map only contaminates the
error bars.

The WLS path composes cleanly with every other ISOSTER feature.
All sampling modes (φ-uniform and ψ-uniform), all harmonic-fit
modes (post-hoc, `'in_loop'` ISOFIT, `'original'` ISOFIT), all
constraint flags (`fix_center`, `fix_pa`, `fix_eps`), the LSB
outskirt features (§1.4.3), the batch-robustness primitives
(§1.4.4), and the multi-band joint free fit (§1.4.5) all carry the
WLS weights through unchanged. The composability is a property of
the implementation: the weighted normal equations are constructed
once per fit (or once per iteration, in the ISOFIT-`'in_loop'`
case) and solved through the same NumPy `linalg.lstsq` pipeline
that handles the OLS solve, with the weight matrix entering as
a row-wise prescaling of $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ before
the call. Adding a new feature that affects the harmonic fit does
not require a parallel WLS implementation; it inherits the
weighted solve automatically.

## §1.4.2.5 Demonstration: LegacySurvey galaxy with inverse-variance map

To make the OLS / WLS difference visible we use a LegacySurvey
(DECaLS) galaxy from the SGA-2020 sample, drawn from the curated
demonstration subset in `sga_isoster/data/demo/` (an external
companion repository, not part of the ISOSTER source tree). The galaxy is a
moderately bright disk-dominated late-type system at intermediate
inclination, large enough that the outward fit pushes well into
the LSB regime where the OLS / WLS distinction becomes
quantitatively significant. The science image is the $r$-band
coadded frame in nanomaggies, and the variance map is constructed
from the LegacySurvey per-pixel inverse-variance ($\mathtt{invvar}$)
extension by

$$
\sigma^2(x, y) = \begin{cases}
1 / \mathtt{invvar}(x, y) & \mathtt{invvar} > 0,\\
\mathrm{NaN} & \mathtt{invvar} = 0,
\end{cases}
$$

following the canonical recipe of §1.4.2.3; ISOSTER marks the
resulting `NaN` entries invalid and drops the corresponding samples
during sampling, rather than folding them into the ring statistic
with a large finite weight.

image with the WLS-fitted isophotes overlaid in cyan. Panel B: the
surface-brightness profile $\mu(a)$ in $\mathrm{mag\,arcsec^{-2}}$
from a single ISOSTER fit, with error bars from two error-bar
*sources* — OLS (red, residual-scatter) and WLS (blue,
variance-map-propagated) — plotted as transparent fills on either
side of the same central curve. The two error envelopes agree in
the inner $\mu \lesssim 25\,\mathrm{mag\,arcsec^{-2}}$ region; in
the LSB outskirts ($\mu \gtrsim 26.5\,\mathrm{mag\,arcsec^{-2}}$)
the WLS envelope is approximately $1.5\times$ to $2\times$ wider
than the OLS envelope, reflecting the underlying per-pixel photon

`sga_isoster/data/demo/` (external companion repository); specific
galaxy ID to be selected at
figure-commission time from the curated subset.]**

The visible difference between the two envelopes in the LSB
outskirts is the headline argument: in the regime where the
photometric science cases are most demanding — disk-truncation
edges, intracluster light, stellar-halo profiles — WLS is providing
a more honest description of how well the fit constrains the
intensity, with no change to the fit geometry itself. In the
high-$S/N$ inner region, the OLS and WLS envelopes coincide, which
confirms that WLS does not generically inflate error bars; it
inflates them only where the OLS estimator's structural bias makes
them too small.

The reproducibility recipe is:

```python
import numpy as np
from astropy.io import fits
from isoster import fit_image
from isoster.config import IsosterConfig

image = fits.getdata("galaxy-r.fits.fz")
invvar = fits.getdata("galaxy-invvar-r.fits.fz")
variance_map = np.where(invvar > 0, 1.0 / invvar, np.nan)

config = IsosterConfig(sma0=10.0, maxsma=200.0,
                       full_photometry=True)

# OLS baseline (no variance map)
results_ols = fit_image(image, mask=mask, config=config)

# WLS fit (same config, variance map supplied)
results_wls = fit_image(image, mask=mask, config=config,
                        variance_map=variance_map)
```

The two `results` dictionaries differ almost entirely in the
`*_err` fields of each isophote. For a roughly uniform variance map
the weights are nearly constant along each ellipse, so the fitted
geometry, the harmonic coefficients, and the curve-of-growth fields
agree to within LAPACK floating-point determinism, as expected from
the high-$S/N$ equivalence argument above; a strongly non-uniform
variance map also shifts the fitted values slightly, because the
weights then change the solve itself. The plotting recipe for the
figure is
to read the two results dictionaries, extract `intens_err` versus
`sma` from each, and overplot the two envelopes on a single
$\mu(a)$ profile.

A final practical point. WLS is essentially free at runtime: the
weighted solve costs the same as the unweighted solve once the row
scaling is applied, and the scaling itself is one NumPy elementwise
operation per isophote. The 25 to 35 percent overhead of the
ISOFIT path (§1.4.1) does not apply here. On a per-galaxy basis
WLS adds a few percent at most to the wall time of a regular
fit, and that few percent is paid back in cleaner error bars and
automatic outlier handling. For survey pipelines processing
thousands of galaxies with already-computed inverse-variance maps,
turning on WLS is the obvious default.
