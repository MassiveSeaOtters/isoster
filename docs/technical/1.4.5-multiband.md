# §1.4.5 Multi-band joint free fit

Modern resolved-galaxy science is almost always *multi-band*. The
photometric pipelines that deliver the imaging — Hyper Suprime-Cam
SSP, DECaLS / BASS / MzLS, DES, the Sloan Digital Sky Survey, the
eventual LSST main survey — provide co-aligned images in three or
more filters per target, each with its own per-pixel inverse-
variance map. The scientific quantities of interest — color
gradients, age maps, dust attenuation profiles, spatially-resolved
stellar-population fits — are not single-band quantities; they
depend on differences and ratios across bands and on whether those
differences are evaluated at *the same physical isophote* in each
band.

The classical multi-band workflow handles this through
*forced photometry*. A reference band is fit independently to
discover the per-SMA geometry, and the other bands are then
extracted under that fixed geometry as forced templates. This is
the workflow ISOSTER supports through its `template` argument
(§1.2). For galaxies whose morphology is genuinely band-independent
— most ellipticals, and most disk galaxies within a fixed
photometric aperture in the inner high-$S/N$ region — forced
photometry is a defensible choice and produces reasonable per-band
intensity profiles.

For galaxies with morphological structure that *varies* across
bands — disk-bulge color gradients, dust-lane asymmetries, AGN
color excess, star-formation regions that appear preferentially in
shorter wavelengths — forced photometry makes a choice that is
worth surfacing rather than leaving implicit: it reports every band
on an aperture sequence selected by one reference band, so the
non-reference bands are measured on ellipses that are not their own
isophotes. That is a real property of the workflow, and it is the
motivation for the *joint multi-band free fit* described in this
subsection. The joint fit solves for a single shared geometry per
SMA across all bands simultaneously, with per-band intensities and
per-band higher-order harmonics as additional unknowns in the
linear system.

It is important not to overstate what that fixes, and §1.4.5.1
develops the point at length. The joint fit does not recover each
band's own isophotes either; it also imposes one shared aperture
sequence, and when the bands genuinely differ in shape that
sequence is a compromise. What changes is *how the compromise is
chosen* — from the data, weighted by each band's geometric
leverage, rather than by the analyst's choice of reference band —
and, decisively for colour work, that the same aperture is used in
every band, so a colour is differenced on identical apertures. The
forced-photometry workflow remains available for the case where a
predetermined aperture is what the science calls for; the joint free
fit is the recommended path for colour-gradient science, on the
strength of the precision result measured in §1.4.5.10.

This subsection describes the joint algorithm. It is the longest
feature subsection because the multi-band fit is algorithmically
distinct enough from single-band that several design choices —
the joint design-matrix structure and what its shared parameters
should be, the four higher-harmonic modes, per-band masking through
loose validity, the per-band Bender normalization — need explicit
treatment. A theme runs through the first, third and fourth of those:
in a multi-band fit, the quantity that is physically common across
bands is a *geometric* or *dimensionless shape* quantity, while the
quantity a naive linear system shares is an *intensity amplitude*.
Every band's amplitude carries that band's own radial gradient, so
sharing amplitudes is a misspecification wherever the gradients
differ. Recognising this in the geometry solve (§1.4.5.2) and in the
shared higher harmonics (§1.4.5.3) is what makes the joint fit
insensitive to the bands' arbitrary flux units.

## §1.4.5.1 What each multi-band workflow actually measures

Consider a disk-bulge galaxy whose disk component dominates the
$g$-band light and whose bulge component dominates the $i$-band
light. The $g$-band isophotes at intermediate SMA are nearly
circular and aligned with the disk's projected geometry. The
$i$-band isophotes at the same intermediate SMA are more elliptical
and aligned with the bulge's photometric major axis. The two
geometries are *physically different* — they reflect the different
spatial distributions of the two stellar populations — and a fit
that allows each band its own geometry finds these differences
without difficulty.

Forced photometry overrides this by enforcing the $g$-band
geometry on the $i$-band extraction. The $i$-band intensity at SMA
$a$ is now reported as the mean intensity along an ellipse that is
*not* an isophote of the $i$-band image; it is an ellipse drawn
along $g$-band isophotal contours and resampled in $i$. The
reported intensity is biased away from the $i$-band's natural
intensity at the *physical* radius defined by the $i$-band's
shape. The bias is small in the inner region where the two bands
are both above the noise floor and their geometries differ
modestly; it grows toward larger SMAs where the geometry
divergence accumulates.

A second, related bias appears in the higher-order harmonics. The
classical forced-photometry workflow extracts only the intensity
along the fixed ellipse; the higher-order harmonics $(A_n, B_n)$
for $n \ge 3$ are either omitted from the forced result or computed
on the same fixed geometry. The latter produces a coefficient
profile that mixes the genuine higher-order morphology of the
$i$-band image with the forced-geometry mismatch at orders 1 and 2
— a contamination that grows with band-to-band morphological
difference.

The joint free fit changes what the shared aperture *is*. It treats
the geometry as a shared unknown across bands and the intensities as
per-band unknowns: the shared geometry is the one geometry per SMA
that minimizes the joint residual across all $B$ bands, and the
per-band intensities are the best-fit zero points of each band's
sampled profile under it. The reported $i$-band intensity at SMA $a$
is therefore not the intensity along the $g$-band's preferred
ellipse; it is the intensity along an ellipse that every band had a
say in, weighted by how much geometric information each carries.

**Three workflows, three different estimands.** It would be
convenient to say that this removes the forced-photometry bias, and
the preceding paragraphs invite that reading, but it does not — and
the distinction is worth drawing sharply, because it determines what
any demonstration can legitimately claim. The three workflows do not
estimate the same quantity:

- **Forced photometry** reports each band's intensity on an aperture
  sequence chosen by one reference band.
- **The joint free fit** reports each band's intensity on one
  data-informed shared aperture sequence.
- **Independent per-band fitting** reports each band on its own
  natural isophotal geometry.

When the bands genuinely differ in morphology, *no* shared-geometry
method can be unbiased with respect to every band's own isophotes,
because there is no single aperture sequence that is right for all
of them. The joint fit returns a compromise just as forced
photometry does; §1.4.5.8 says so explicitly. What differs is how
the compromise is chosen — by the data, in proportion to each band's
geometric leverage, rather than by the analyst's choice of reference
band — and, decisively for colour work, that the compromise is
*common to all bands*, so a colour formed by differencing two of
them is differenced on identical apertures.

That last property is the durable argument, and it is the one the
measured experiment of §1.4.5.10 supports: what the joint fit
demonstrably buys is *precision* in the colour profile at low
signal-to-noise, where independent per-band geometries scatter and
their difference mixes a real colour gradient with a spurious
geometry mismatch. The claim that the joint fit is *unbiased* under
genuinely band-dependent morphology is a separate proposition; it
would require a separate experiment, on a mock with genuinely
band-dependent structure, and that experiment has not been
performed.

## §1.4.5.2 The joint design matrix

The single-band fit at each iteration constructs the
five-parameter design matrix

$$
\mathbf{A}_5 = [\mathbf{1}, \sin\theta, \cos\theta, \sin 2\theta, \cos 2\theta] \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 5}
$$

with $N$ sample points along the trial ellipse, and solves the
linear system $\mathbf{A}_5\,\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}$ for the
parameter vector $\mathbf{x} = (I_0, A_1, B_1, A_2, B_2)$.

The joint multi-band fit at each iteration constructs an extended
matrix in which the per-band intensity column $\mathbf{1}$ is
replaced by $B$ band-specific columns (each non-zero only on the
$N$ rows corresponding to that band), and the four geometry
harmonic columns are vertically tiled across all bands:

$$
\mathbf{A}_\mathrm{MB} = \left[\begin{array}{cccc|cccc}
\mathbf{1} & 0          & \cdots & 0          & \sin\theta & \cos\theta & \sin 2\theta & \cos 2\theta \\
0          & \mathbf{1} & \cdots & 0          & \sin\theta & \cos\theta & \sin 2\theta & \cos 2\theta \\
\vdots     & \vdots     & \ddots & \vdots     & \vdots     & \vdots     & \vdots       & \vdots       \\
0          & 0          & \cdots & \mathbf{1} & \sin\theta & \cos\theta & \sin 2\theta & \cos 2\theta
\end{array}\right]
\in \mathbb{R}^{B N \times (B + 4)}.
$$

The first $B$ columns are *per-band intensity zero points*: column
$b$ has the value $1$ on the $N$ rows corresponding to band $b$
and zero everywhere else. The last four columns are the *shared
geometry harmonic terms*, repeated identically on every band's
$N$-row block. The full matrix has $B N$ rows (one per sample
point per band) and $B + 4$ columns.

The corresponding parameter vector is

$$
\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{MB} = (I_{0,1}, I_{0,2}, \ldots, I_{0,B}, A_1, B_1, A_2, B_2).
$$

The first $B$ components are the per-band intensity zero points;
the last four are the shared geometry-mismatch harmonics. The
geometry-update rule of §1.1 reads the four shared coefficients
$(A_1, B_1, A_2, B_2)$ identically to the single-band case: the
dominant amplitude
$a_\max = \max(|A_1|, |B_1|, |A_2|, |B_2|)$ selects which geometry
coordinate to update, and the magnitude $a_\max$ is converted to a
geometry-update step via the local radial gradient. The per-band
intensities are reported as the converged $I_{0,b}$ values in the
output dictionary.

The shape of the joint system has two important properties. First,
the per-band block-diagonal structure of the intensity columns
makes the linear system *well-conditioned* even at large $B$: the
diagonal blocks of $\mathbf{A}_\mathrm{MB}^\mathsf{T}
\mathbf{A}_\mathrm{MB}$ that couple the per-band intensities to the
shared harmonics have rank $4$ in the harmonic sub-block and rank
$B$ in the intensity sub-block, and the intensity–intensity cross
terms vanish identically because the per-band intensity columns are
mutually orthogonal by construction. (The intensity–harmonic cross
terms vanish only for complete, uniformly sampled rings, where
$\sum_j \sin n\theta_j = \sum_j \cos n\theta_j = 0$; on masked or
loose-validity rings they are nonzero and the joint solve absorbs
them.) The conditioning is therefore no worse
than the single-band problem at large $B$. Second, the WLS weights
of §1.4.2 extend transparently to the multi-band case: the weight
matrix $\mathbf{W}_\mathrm{MB} = \mathrm{diag}(1/\sigma_{b,i}^2)$
encodes the per-band per-pixel variances on the corresponding rows
of the system, and the weighted normal equations are constructed
through the same row-scaling pipeline as in single-band.

With $B = 1$ and $w_b = 1$ the joint system reduces to the
single-band five-parameter problem exactly, and the reduction is
verified against the single-band solver rather than asserted.

### What the shared parameters should be: amplitudes or geometry steps

The system written above shares a harmonic *amplitude* across bands,
and that turns out to be the wrong thing to share. A harmonic
amplitude is not a geometric quantity: a common geometry error
$\delta$ — the same misplacement of the same ellipse, seen in every
band — produces a ring harmonic whose amplitude in band $b$ is
$\delta \cdot (dI/da)_b$, proportional to *that band's* radial
gradient. Bands with different gradients therefore see the same
geometric error at different amplitudes, and a model that forces one
amplitude on all of them is misspecified whenever the gradients
differ. What such a fit returns is $\delta$ multiplied by a
weight-averaged gradient, which then has to be divided back out by a
separately pooled gradient before the geometry can be updated — two
quantities that have no structural reason to agree.

ISOSTER's default therefore fits the geometry steps directly. Each
band's four shared columns are scaled by that band's own gradient, so
the model becomes

$$
y_{b,i} = I_{0,b} + \left(\frac{dI}{da}\right)_b
\left(D_1 \sin\theta_i + E_1 \cos\theta_i
    + D_2 \sin 2\theta_i + E_2 \cos 2\theta_i\right),
$$

and the four shared free parameters $(D_1, E_1, D_2, E_2)$ are the
geometry steps themselves. The per-band intercept columns are never
scaled — $I_{0,b}$ remains an intensity. This is the
`geometry_parameterized_solve` field, and it has been the default
since 2026-08-15; setting it `False` recovers the shared-amplitude
form above, which is also what the first iteration of each isophote
uses, since it has no previous-iteration gradients to scale with.

The reparameterisation removes a defect rather than trading one off,
in three separate ways:

- **The fitted step no longer depends on a separate gradient
  estimate.** Under the amplitude form the recovered geometry step is
  correct only if the pooled gradient happens to reproduce the
  weight-average the solve implicitly performed; here the step is
  what the solve returns. ISOSTER still multiplies by a pooled
  gradient afterwards, but only to hand downstream consumers the
  units they expect, and the geometry update divides by the same
  number, so the round trip is exact by construction rather than by
  coincidence.
- **The effective per-band weight becomes minimum-variance.** Under
  the amplitude form each band enters the geometry constraint with
  weight $w_b / \sigma_b^2$; under the geometry form it enters with
  $w_b (dI/da)_b^2 / \sigma_b^2$, which is the correct weighting for
  estimating a geometry step, because a band's leverage on the
  geometry scales with its gradient. The difference is not academic:
  measured on HSC $g/r/i$ imaging, the amplitude weighting was close
  to *inverted* against the bands' actual information content, giving
  the $g$ band 65% of the weight while it carried about 10% of the
  information. Against synthetic data with a known common geometry,
  the geometry form leaves the bias unchanged and cuts the scatter of
  the recovered geometry by roughly 25–30% wherever the two
  weightings disagree, and changes nothing where they agree. It also
  improves the OLS case, where the amplitude form weights every band
  equally regardless of how much geometric signal each carries.
- **The shared coefficients and the residual scatter end up in the
  same units**, so the convergence comparison stops depending on any
  band's arbitrary flux scale.

A band whose gradient is zero or unmeasurable contributes zero
columns and therefore no geometry information, which is the correct
behaviour rather than a special case: no measurable radial gradient
means no geometric leverage.

Downstream of the solve, ISOSTER converts the fitted steps back into
amplitude-equivalent coefficients by multiplying the shared block by
the same pooled gradient the geometry update will divide by, so the
update recovers the fitted step exactly and every consumer — the
dominant-amplitude selector, the convergence test, the covariance-
based geometry errors, the higher-harmonic attachers — keeps working
in the units it already expected. Only the *value* changes, and it
now carries the minimum-variance band weighting.

That conversion introduces one implementation invariant worth naming,
because violating it is silent. Band $b$'s *fitted* amplitude is
$\delta \cdot (dI/da)_b$, not $\delta \cdot \overline{dI/da}$, so
anything that reconstructs the model for band $b$ must apply the
per-band factor $(dI/da)_b / \overline{dI/da}$. That includes the
per-band residuals, the convergence RMS built from them, the OLS
error rescaling, and the harmonic subtraction that the shared and
simultaneous higher-order modes perform. Omitting the factor leaves
the geometry step itself exact while making every residual-derived
quantity wrong, which is precisely the kind of error that survives a
test suite aimed at the fitted parameters.

### Pooling the gradient with the weights the solve used

The geometry update needs a single radial gradient, and the multi-band
driver forms it by pooling the per-band gradients. The pooling weights
must be the weights the harmonic solve actually applied, not the
user-facing band weights. The joint solve weights every sample by
$w_b / \sigma_{b,i}^2$, so band $b$ contributes total weight

$$
W_b = w_b \sum_{i} \frac{1}{\sigma_{b,i}^2},
$$

and the shared fitted amplitude under the amplitude parameterisation
is $\delta \sum_b W_b (dI/da)_b / \sum_b W_b$. Dividing that by a
gradient pooled with the *same* $W_b$ returns $\delta$ exactly;
pooling with the bare $w_b$ does not, and the resulting error is not
even one-signed — against a planted truth of $0.100$, two different
band configurations recovered $0.180$ and $0.045$. Under OLS there is
no variance map, the solve weights by $w_b$ alone, and $W_b$ reduces
to $w_b$. The variances used must also be the *post-clipping* arrays
handed to the solve; taking them from the sampler's unfiltered ring
would put the pooled gradient and the fitted amplitude back on
different sample sets, which is the class of error the whole
construction exists to remove.

One honest limitation. The exactly correct weight is
$\sum_i w_b \sin^2\theta_i / \sigma_{b,i}^2$, which differs per
harmonic once the variance varies *azimuthally within* a band, so no
single scalar can be right for $A_1, B_1, A_2, B_2$ simultaneously.
$W_b$ as defined above is exact for shared angular coverage with an
azimuthally uniform variance inside each band — the common case,
where bands differ in sky level but each band's own ring is uniform —
and an approximation otherwise. Under
`harmonic_combination='ref'` the geometry is driven by one band, and
the pooling collapses to a one-hot weight on that band, so the
reference band's own gradient is the one used.

### Configuration fields that reshape the solve

Four configuration fields change either the design matrix or the
weighting, and are worth naming together.
`geometry_parameterized_solve` (default `True`) selects the
geometry-step parameterisation described above over the
shared-amplitude form. `harmonic_combination` selects how the
harmonic information enters the geometry update: `'joint'` (default)
solves the single combined system once per iteration, while `'ref'`
drives the geometry from the reference band alone and treats the
other bands as passive. `fit_per_band_intens_jointly` (default
`True`) keeps the $B$ per-band intercept columns inside the joint
solve as shown; setting it `False` drops those columns, runs the
geometric solve on mean-subtracted residuals, and computes each
`intens_<b>` separately as that band's own ring statistic. The two
agree numerically on complete rings, where $\sin n\theta$ and
$\cos n\theta$ are orthogonal to the constant column over
$[0, 2\pi]$; they differ on partial rings, where the joint solve
absorbs the coupling between the intercept and the harmonic shape.
The decoupled mode is also the only one that can host
`integrator='median'`, since a median cannot ride inside a linear
least-squares solve; the configuration validator rejects that
combination outright rather than silently reporting a mean.
`band_weights` applies a per-band scalar weight $w_b$ to each
band's row block of the normal equations (uniform by default), so
a higher-$S/N$ band can be given more influence over the shared
geometry.

A practical implementation note. The construction of
$\mathbf{A}_\mathrm{MB}$ for $B$ in the typical survey range (3–6
bands) and $N$ in the typical isophote range (64–600 sample points)
produces matrices with $B N$ in the range $200$–$3600$ — small
enough that a vectorized NumPy construction is fast in absolute
terms, but large enough that the per-iteration overhead would be
noticeable without optimization. ISOSTER implements
$\mathbf{A}_\mathrm{MB}$ construction in a Numba-JIT inner kernel
(`build_joint_design_matrix` in `isoster/multiband/numba_kernels_mb.py`)
that fills the block structure directly without going through
intermediate NumPy reshape operations. A byte-equivalent NumPy
fallback is available for systems without Numba, as elsewhere in
the codebase.

## §1.4.5.3 Four modes for higher-order harmonics

The single-band ISOFIT path of §1.4.1 has two configurable axes:
*when* the higher-order harmonics are fit (inside the iteration
loop vs. post-hoc on the converged geometry) and *which* design
matrix is used (the 5-parameter geometry-only matrix vs. the
extended matrix that includes higher orders). The multi-band path
adds a third axis: *whether the higher-order harmonics are shared
across bands* or fit independently per band.

The Cartesian product of the three axes would have eight modes,
but only four are scientifically distinct and operationally
supported. They are exposed through the `multiband_higher_harmonics`
configuration field:

**`'independent'` (default).** Higher-order harmonics are fit
per-band post-hoc. After the iteration loop converges on the
shared geometry, ISOSTER performs $B$ separate post-hoc fits, one
per band, each using the standard single-band higher-order
harmonic procedure. The resulting coefficient set is
$(A_{n,b}, B_{n,b})$ for each order $n \in \{n_1, \ldots, n_L\}$
and each band $b$. This is the right default for color-gradient
science because the per-band higher harmonics are genuinely
different physical quantities — a $g$-band spiral arm may be
absent in $i$-band, a dust lane may produce different fourth-order
coefficients in different bands, an AGN's color excess affects
only specific bands. Reporting band-distinct coefficients is the
correct generalization of the single-band default to the
multi-band case.

**`'shared'`.** A single post-hoc joint fit over all bands is
performed after convergence, on a residual design matrix that
contains only the higher-order harmonic columns; the geometric
$(A_1, B_1, A_2, B_2)$ and the per-band intercepts $I_{0,b}$ stay
frozen at their converged values, and the residuals subtract that
frozen geometric model band by band.

What the mode shares is a *shape*, not a raw amplitude, and the
distinction is the same one that drove the geometry parameterisation
of §1.4.5.2. A common dimensionless shape deviation produces a raw
amplitude $A_n^\mathrm{raw} = -a_n \cdot a \cdot
(dI/da)_b$ in band $b$ — again proportional to that band's gradient.
Writing one identical raw value into every band therefore cannot
represent one shared shape once the gradients differ, and because the
Bender normalization of §1.4.5.5 divides by each band's own gradient,
such a fit reports normalized amplitudes that are band-dependent
*and* dependent on the bands' arbitrary flux units. ISOSTER
consequently scales each band's higher-order columns by
$-a\,(dI/da)_b$, so the fitted coefficient *is* the dimensionless
Bender-normalized amplitude, and converts back to each band's own raw
units when writing Schema 1. The per-band raw columns then
legitimately differ — that is what one shared shape looks like in raw
units — and the invariant to check is that they all *normalize* to a
single value.

The improvement is measurable on a planted shape of $0.02$ in two
bands, rescaling one band's arbitrary flux units:

| band units | reported normalized $a_4$, band $g$ | band $r$ |
|---|---|---|
| original | 0.02000 | 0.02000 |
| band $r$ × 10 | 0.11000 | 0.01100 |
| band $r$ × 0.1 | 0.01100 | 0.11000 |

Under the shape parameterisation both bands report $0.02000$ at every
rescaling. The mode is the right choice when the user *assumes* the
higher-order shape parameters are band-independent — typical of
kinematic-isophote analyses where the shape is set by the stellar
orbital structure rather than by the photometric distribution.

**`'simultaneous_in_loop'`.** The full multi-band ISOFIT analogue
of §1.4.1's `isofit_mode='in_loop'`. Inside every iteration of the
geometry fit, the design matrix is extended from $(B N) \times (B + 4)$
to $(B N) \times (B + 4 + 2L)$ by appending the $2L$ shared higher-
harmonic columns at the right. The joint solve absorbs the
cross-correlations between geometry harmonics and higher-order
harmonics throughout the iteration. This is the most aggressive
multi-band variant and the most computationally expensive; it is
aimed at galaxies with strong non-elliptical isophotal structure
where the higher harmonics interact with the geometry update.

**`'simultaneous_original'`.** The Ciambur (2015) original
variant. The iteration loop uses the standard $(B N) \times (B + 4)$
design matrix; after convergence, the wider $(B N) \times (B + 4 + 2L)$
matrix is solved once. The geometry that the iteration converges to
is identical to the `'independent'` and `'shared'` modes (since the
inside-loop solve is the same); only the higher-order coefficients
reported are different — they come from the post-convergence joint
solve rather than from per-band post-hoc fits or the `'shared'`
post-hoc residual fit.

**Both `simultaneous_*` modes remain experimental**, for two
independent reasons. The first is inherited: the single-band
equivalent (`simultaneous_harmonics=True`) has shown benchmark
regressions, and it is not established that the joint constraint
rescues it. The second is specific to multi-band and follows directly
from the `'shared'` discussion above — these two modes stamp *one
identical raw amplitude* into every band, so what they share is a raw
intensity residual, not a shape. They therefore no longer agree with
`'shared'` column by column, and their raw coefficients do not
normalize to a common value once the per-band gradients differ.
Repairing them is entangled with the geometry rescale of §1.4.5.2 and
has been deferred; both emit a warning at configuration time, and
`'shared'` is the lower-risk choice for anyone who wants cross-band
coupling of the higher orders. The default `'independent'` mode is
unaffected by all of this: it fits each band separately from that
band's own gradient and was always self-consistent.

The four modes share a single output schema. Whichever mode is
selected, the output isophote dictionary carries per-band columns
named `a<n>_<b>` and `b<n>_<b>` for each band $b$ and order $n$,
with `a<n>_err_<b>` and `b<n>_err_<b>` companions. The columns are
not, however, in the same *units* in every mode, and this is the one
place where a shared schema hides a real difference that consumers
must respect. Under `'independent'` the stored values are already
Bender-normalized, because the per-band post-hoc path uses the
single-band deviation solver, which normalizes at fit time. Under
`'shared'` and the two `'simultaneous_*'` modes the stored values are
*raw*, and the normalization of §1.4.5.5 is applied at plotting time.
The distinction is carried explicitly in the output as the
`harmonics_shared` flag, and ISOSTER's own plotting code reads that
flag to decide whether to normalize. A consumer that normalizes
`'independent'` output a second time will produce silently wrong
curves, which is why the flag is part of the result dictionary rather
than something to be inferred from the mode name.

Under `'shared'` the per-band raw columns differ from one another by
each band's $-a\,(dI/da)_b$ factor; under `'simultaneous_*'` they are
identical across bands; under `'independent'` they are per-band fits.
Only in the first and third cases do the normalized curves carry the
intended meaning.

## §1.4.5.4 Loose validity for per-band masking

The joint design matrix construction of §1.4.5.2 assumes that all
$B$ bands have the same *validity* at every sample point — that
is, that a sample point either has valid (unmasked) pixels in
every band or is excluded from all bands. This is the
*rectangular validity* layout, and it is the right default for
co-aligned, same-grid imaging where the per-pixel masks are
typically also same-grid and band-independent.

Survey pipelines occasionally violate this assumption. A defect
mask in one band that does not appear in the others, a cosmic-ray
hit on one frame that was rejected only in one band's coaddition,
a band-specific bad-column flag — all of these produce per-band
validity that varies across bands. The naive rectangular treatment
would either propagate the mask to all bands (losing information
in the unaffected bands) or ignore the mask in some bands (using
known-bad data).

ISOSTER's *loose validity* mode (`loose_validity=True` in
`IsosterConfigMB`) handles this by replacing the rectangular
$(B, N)$ validity layout with a jagged per-band layout. Each band
maintains its own list of valid sample points, and the joint
design matrix is constructed band-by-band with each band's row
block sized to that band's actual valid count $N_b$:

$$
\mathbf{A}_\mathrm{MB}^\mathrm{loose} \in \mathbb{R}^{\sum_b N_b \times (B + 4)}.
$$

A second JIT-compiled kernel (`build_joint_design_matrix_jagged` in
the same module) handles this jagged construction with NumPy
fallback. The shared geometry harmonics on each band's row block
are evaluated at that band's surviving $\theta$ values, and the
WLS weights are constructed on the same jagged layout from the
per-band variance maps.

A small number of caveats apply to the loose-validity path. First,
the loose-validity construction is meaningful only when the
band-to-band validity disagreement is *sparse*: a band that has
lost most of its samples contributes little to the joint geometry
constraint, and the user should consider whether that band should
participate in the joint fit at all. The configuration fields
`loose_validity_min_per_band_count` (default 6 samples, mirroring the
five-parameter minimum plus one sample of slack) and
`loose_validity_min_per_band_frac` (default $0.2$ of the sampled
count) set the minimum participation below which a band is dropped
from the joint solve at that isophote. A dropped band's `intens_<b>`
and harmonic columns are reported as `NaN` — an explicit "not
measured here", distinguishable from a measured zero — while the
surviving bands still constrain the shared geometry. The isophote as
a whole is failed only when fewer than two bands survive. For the
same reason, `loose_validity=True` is rejected at configuration time
when only one band is supplied: per-band validity has no meaning when
bands cannot disagree, and every isophote would otherwise fail.
Second, the loose-validity weight normalization through the
`loose_validity_band_normalization` field controls whether bands
with smaller $N_b$ are reweighted to contribute equally to the
joint geometry constraint, or whether they are left to contribute
proportionally to their surviving sample count. The default is the
proportional weighting, which is the right choice for typical
band-asymmetric masking; the alternative (`'per_band_count'`)
row-scales each band's block by $\sqrt{1/N_b}$ so that a
user-specified `band_weights` keeps its intended meaning even when
masking has left the bands with very different sample counts.

**Status.** Loose validity is correct end to end and covered by tests
— the forced-photometry path honours it, the joint gradient is
sampled in the same validity mode the solve uses, and coupled
intercepts keep their joint covariance — but it is *not* the default.
Turning it on changes every result on data with per-band masks, so
the switch is deliberately left to the user. The reason to consider
it is quantitative: on the HSC demonstration data, the shared-validity
AND routinely discards 18–27% of otherwise usable samples, and at one
radius it discarded all of them. That is the cost of the rectangular
layout, paid on data where only one band was actually affected.

## §1.4.5.5 Per-band Bender normalization

The normalized higher-harmonic coefficient
$a_n = A_n^\mathrm{raw} / (a\,\lvert dI/da\rvert)$ — the Bender form
$-A_n^\mathrm{raw}/(a\,dI/da)$ wherever the gradient is negative — is the
implementation-independent quantity that quantifies the isophotal
shape deviation; it is the form in which higher-order coefficients
are reported in the isophote-analysis literature. The
normalization factor $1 / (a \cdot dI/da)$ converts the raw
coefficient (which has intensity units that scale with the galaxy
profile) into a dimensionless shape parameter that is comparable
across SMAs and across galaxies.

In the multi-band fit the question arises of *which* gradient to use
in the normalization, because the per-band gradients $(dI/da)_b$ are
different by construction — the per-band intensity profiles differ,
which is the whole reason for fitting multiple bands. ISOSTER's
convention is that the normalization is **always evaluated per band**,
with that band's own gradient:

$$
a_{n,b} = -\,\frac{A_{n,b}^\mathrm{raw}}{a \cdot (dI/da)_b}
\qquad\text{(plot-time, multi-band raw paths).}
$$

This is the *signed* form, applied by `_normalize_harmonic_for_plot` to the
`shared` and `simultaneous_*` paths, whose stored columns are raw. It is not
the expression used by `compute_deviations`, which normalizes at fit time with
the absolute gradient, $a_n = A_n^\mathrm{raw} / (a\,\lvert dI/da\rvert)$;
the two agree wherever the gradient is negative.

This is the right convention because the *meaning* of the normalized
amplitude in band $b$ is a fractional **radial** displacement — how far
the isophote is pushed in or out relative to its own semi-major axis —
measured through band $b$'s own gradient. It is not a fractional
intensity deviation, though the two are easy to confuse: dividing an
intensity residual by the local gradient is precisely what converts it
from a brightness into a displacement.

The convention has a consequence that is easy to state and was easy
to get wrong: it fixes what "shared" has to mean. If a mode intends
to share a shape across bands, then dividing each band's stored raw
value by that band's own gradient must return the *same* number in
every band. The only way to arrange that is for the shared quantity
to be the normalized coefficient itself, with the raw per-band values
reconstructed from it — which is what §1.4.5.3 describes for
`'shared'`. The converse arrangement, storing one identical raw value
in every band, fails the test: dividing one number by $B$ different
gradients gives $B$ different normalized amplitudes, so the reported
"shared" shape becomes a function of the bands' arbitrary flux units.
The two `simultaneous_*` modes still work that way and are flagged
accordingly.

For the default `'independent'` mode the question does not arise: each
band's coefficients are fit from that band's own samples and gradient,
and are stored already normalized.

Where the normalization is *applied* depends on the storage path, and
it is worth being exact because two of the three consumers do nothing at
all. `_normalize_harmonic_for_plot` in `isoster/_shared.py` is called
**only** by the multi-band plotter, and only when the result's
`harmonics_shared` flag says the stored values are raw. The single-band
plotter does not call it — its values were normalized at fit time — and
neither does the audit framework in
`benchmarks/exhausted/analysis/scenario_summary.py`, which consumes the
stored columns directly. The rule is normalize exactly once, wherever
that has not already happened. This consistency is essential for
reproducibility: a published $a_n$ value from an
ISOSTER-fit galaxy can be recomputed from the saved isophote
dictionary plus a single band-identifier label.

## §1.4.5.6 Output schema and serialization

The multi-band output dictionary mirrors the single-band structure
with additions. Alongside the `isophotes` key (a list of per-SMA
dictionaries) and the resolved `IsosterConfigMB`, the top level
records everything a consumer needs to interpret the per-band columns
without re-reading the configuration: the ordered `bands` list, the
`reference_band`, the resolved `band_weights`, the `variance_mode`
(`'wls'` or `'ols'`), the `harmonic_combination`, the
`multiband_higher_harmonics` mode with its `harmonic_orders`, the
`loose_validity` and `fit_per_band_intens_jointly` switches, and the
`harmonics_shared` boolean of §1.4.5.3. Each per-SMA dictionary
carries:

- The single shared geometry block: `sma`, `x0`, `y0`, `eps`, `pa`
  and their `_err` companions; the stop code; the iteration count;
  the joint `rms`; and the shared sample-count diagnostics `ndata`
  and `nflag`. Under shared validity those two are the ring's kept
  and sigma-clipped counts; under loose validity, where the bands no
  longer share a sample set, they are sums over bands.
- Per-band intensity blocks: `intens_<b>`, `intens_err_<b>`,
  `rms_<b>`, and `n_valid_<b>` — the last being that band's own
  surviving-sample count after sigma clipping, which equals the
  shared `ndata` under shared validity and diverges from it under
  loose validity.
- Per-band higher-harmonic blocks: `a<n>_<b>`, `b<n>_<b>`,
  `a<n>_err_<b>`, `b<n>_err_<b>` for each order
  $n \in$ `harmonic_orders` and each band $b$ — the multi-band
  Schema 1 layout. As §1.4.5.3 sets out, these are raw amplitudes
  under `'shared'` and the `'simultaneous_*'` modes and already
  Bender-normalized under `'independent'`; `harmonics_shared`
  distinguishes the two cases. The column *names* are the same in
  all modes.
- Optional per-band curve-of-growth columns (`cog_<b>`,
  `cog_annulus_<b>`) with shared `area_annulus` and crossing flags,
  when `compute_cog=True`.
- Optional per-band gradient diagnostics (`grad_<b>`,
  `grad_error_<b>`, `grad_r_error_<b>`) when `debug=True`.

Note the suffix ordering: the band identifier always comes *last*, so
an error column is `intens_err_g`, not `intens_g_err`. The rule makes
a band's columns share a common suffix, which is what lets a consumer
select one band's block with a single string match.

Two serialization paths are provided. The `isophote_results_mb_to_asdf`
writer is the recommended path for multi-band output because ASDF
natively supports nested dicts and the per-band column proliferation
is more readable in ASDF's tree structure than as FITS BinTable
columns. A FITS path through `isophote_results_mb_to_fits` is also
available; it produces a four-HDU file (primary, `ISOPHOTES`,
`CONFIG`, `META`) analogous to the single-band layout, with the
per-band columns flattened into the `ISOPHOTES` BinTable, the
multi-band identifiers in the primary header, and the remaining
top-level result keys preserved as JSON rows in `META` so they
survive the round trip. Both writers live in
`isoster/multiband/utils_mb.py` and share a common helper layer with
the single-band serializers in `isoster/_shared.py`, ensuring that the
configuration round-trip property of §1.3.4 — the saved `CONFIG`
block recording every resolved field, so the settings need not be
supplied out of band — applies to multi-band fits as well, with the
same scope: the file records the configuration, not the images,
software version or numerical environment.

## §1.4.5.7 Parallel multi-band command-line interface

The single-band CLI `isoster` and the multi-band CLI `isoster-mb`
are *parallel*: they share no Python implementation, by design. The
multi-band CLI is defined in `isoster/multiband/cli_mb.py` and is
exposed as the entry point `isoster-mb` in `pyproject.toml`. The
isolation is deliberate. Multi-band fitting carries operational
risks — per-band mask asymmetry, missing variance maps in some
bands, alignment failures — that have no counterpart in single-band,
and a shared CLI implementation would have required threading
multi-band-specific error handling and validation through the
single-band code path. The parallel CLI keeps each path's failure
modes localized to its own implementation.

A typical invocation looks like:

```bash
isoster-mb \
  image_g.fits image_r.fits image_i.fits \
  --variance-maps variance_g.fits variance_r.fits variance_i.fits \
  --mask    mask.fits \
  --bands g r i \
  --config config.yaml \
  --output galaxy_mb.asdf
```

The CLI takes $B$ co-aligned science images as positional
arguments, optionally $B$ corresponding variance maps via
`--variance-maps` (each with the same shape as its science image),
an optional single shared mask via `--mask` (or $B$ per-band masks
via `--masks` for the loose-validity path), and an
`IsosterConfigMB` in YAML form. The output is a single ASDF file
with the full multi-band schema. The CLI prints a banner on each
invocation (suppressible with a flag) warning that the multi-band
API and output schema may still change between releases — a
statement about interface stability, not about the correctness of
the fit; see the maturity note in §1.4.5.8.

## §1.4.5.8 Caveats and design choices worth flagging

**Maturity, stated precisely.** The default multi-band configuration
— shared validity, joint per-band intercepts, the
geometry-parameterised solve, and `'independent'` higher harmonics, in
either OLS or WLS — is supported rather than experimental. Three
qualifications attach to that, and they are of three different kinds,
which the previous phrasing ran together.

*One mode is known to be wrong for its stated purpose.* The two
`'simultaneous_*'` higher-harmonic modes (§1.4.5.3) share a raw
residual rather than a shape, and carry a warning for that reason
among others.

*One correct feature is not the default.* `loose_validity=True`
(§1.4.5.4) is correct and tested end to end; it is off by default
because turning it on changes every result on data with per-band
masks, which is a decision for the user rather than a defect in the
mode.

*One dimension is under-evidenced rather than unsupported.* The
five-band case exercises no different code path from the three-band
case, but at the time of writing it has synthetic coverage only,
because the five-band
real-data cutouts were not available on the development machine.
Real-data validation is otherwise concentrated on a small number of
targets. Drawing the line here rather than labelling the whole feature
"experimental" is the honest description: the parts that are exercised
are exercised hard, and the parts that are not are named.

Six further caveats apply to the joint fit itself:

**Bands must be co-aligned on the same pixel grid.** The joint
design matrix assumes a single $\theta$-parametrization that maps
to identical $(x, y)$ pixel coordinates across all bands. If the
bands have different WCS, the caller must resample to a common
grid before the call; the driver itself performs a shape-based
validation only, so WCS-level alignment is the caller's
responsibility.

**The shared geometry is the *same* geometry, not an *average*
geometry.** The joint fit converges on one set of geometric
parameters per SMA, chosen to minimize the joint residual across
bands. This is not a per-band geometry averaged across bands. A
galaxy with truly band-distinct geometries (e.g. an AGN with
position-angle differences between optical and infrared bands)
will produce a joint-fit geometry that is some compromise of the
per-band geometries, weighted by the per-band $S/N$. For such
galaxies, the user should consider whether single-band independent
fits or a more sophisticated multi-component model is the
appropriate analysis.

**The `'shared'` and `'simultaneous_*'` modes assume band-independent
isophotal shape.** As above for the geometry, the modes that couple
higher-order harmonics across bands are correct only when the
shape deviations are actually band-independent. The default
`'independent'` mode is the safe choice when this assumption is
not verified.

**Per-band gradients enter the default solve as scale factors.** The
geometry parameterisation of §1.4.5.2 scales each band's shared
columns by that band's radial gradient, taken from the previous
iteration and refreshed as the fit proceeds. These are slowly varying
nuisance quantities, not fitted parameters, and the first iteration of
each isophote falls back to the amplitude form because no previous
gradient exists yet. The practical consequence is that a band whose
gradient is unmeasurable — deep in the noise, or on a ring where the
profile is locally flat — silently contributes no geometric
information at that isophote. That is the correct behaviour, but it
means the effective number of bands constraining the geometry can be
smaller than $B$ in the outskirts, which is worth knowing when
interpreting the geometry errors there.

**The loose-validity path is a generalization that has its own
risks.** As discussed in §1.4.5.4, very sparse per-band validity
weakens the joint geometry constraint from the affected band. The
caller should set the `loose_validity_min_per_band_*` thresholds
to enforce a sensible minimum participation per band.

**Computational cost grows linearly with $B$ in the per-iteration
sampling and design-matrix construction, and with $B$ in the
intensity-block dimension of the linear solve.** For
$B \le 6$ (the typical survey case) the per-iteration cost is a
small multiple of the single-band cost; for $B$ in the
tens-of-bands range (the eventual narrowband-survey case), the
joint fit becomes a more significant computational item and the
caller should consider whether the higher-band variants are best
fit with a multi-pass strategy over reduced band subsets, with
forced photometry between subsets. Loose validity does not help
here: its jagged system has the same $B + 4$ columns and typically
*more* rows, since it stops discarding samples that only one band
failed.

## §1.4.5.10 When the joint fit matters: a signal-to-noise dependence

No experiment has yet been run on a mock with genuinely
band-dependent morphology, so nothing so far establishes how the
three workflows compare in that regime. What *has* been measured is
a different
and more tractable question: with the geometry genuinely common
across bands, how much does fitting it jointly improve the recovered
colour profile? The answer is a strong function of
signal-to-noise, and it is the quantitative result this section
rests on.

The test galaxy is constructed so that both the geometry and the
color profile are analytic. Every band shares one geometry
($x_0, y_0, \varepsilon, \mathrm{PA}$) by construction, and the
color gradient is produced by giving each band its own effective
radius, so the bands differ in radial profile and in radial
gradient while the isophote *shape* is identical. Per-band noise
follows measured HSC $g/r/i$ sky variances. The joint free fit is
compared against the pre-multi-band workflow — fit each band
independently, then difference the resulting profiles — by the RMS
error of the recovered $g-i$ profile against the analytic truth,
over independent noise realisations.

| S/N per pixel at the outer radius | joint RMS (mag) | independent RMS (mag) | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 81.9 | 0.00149 | 0.00167 | 0.896 |
| 8.2 | 0.00236 | 0.00477 | **0.494** |
| 2.7 | 0.00635 | 0.01213 | **0.523** |
| 1.4 | 0.01149 | 0.02174 | **0.529** |

The joint fit roughly **halves** the color-profile error once the
outer isophotes fall below $\mathrm{S/N} \sim 10$, and buys almost
nothing above $\mathrm{S/N} \sim 50$.

This shape is the expected one, and stating it is more useful than
quoting a single ratio. At high signal-to-noise every band already
constrains its own geometry well, the independently fitted
geometries agree, and a shared geometry has nothing left to
contribute. At low signal-to-noise the per-band geometries scatter,
and a color formed by differencing them mixes the real color
gradient with a spurious geometry mismatch that the joint
constraint removes. Low signal-to-noise is precisely the regime
where color profiles are scientifically interesting and
observationally hard — the outskirts of galaxies, low-surface-
brightness structure, and the faint end of any survey — so the
regime where the method helps is the regime where it is needed.

It also bounds the claim honestly. A reader fitting high-S/N
imaging of a bright galaxy should not expect the joint fit to
change their color profile, and should choose it for the
consistency of the shared aperture rather than for precision.
