# §1.6 Limitations and roadmap

The technical chapter closes with an honest accounting of where
ISOSTER currently falls short and where the development effort is
heading. The limitations described below are not failures relative
to a putative ideal tool; they are the boundaries of what ISOSTER
covers in the version this paper documents. Some are intrinsic to
the algorithmic scope (2-D images, non-parametric isophote
analysis); some are operational (compute cost on cold starts,
calibration of LSB-regime defaults); some are deferred (GPU
kernels, IFU cube support). The roadmap items at the end of the
subsection name the development directions we anticipate
addressing in subsequent releases, without committing to a release
schedule.

The honest-accounting framing matters for the same reason the
per-feature demonstration sections in §1.4 were cherry-picked but
honest: a tool that overstates its scope produces application
failures downstream, and a tool that understates its scope is
under-used. The aim of this subsection is to leave the reader with
an accurate map of what ISOSTER will and will not do on a given
problem.

## §1.6.1 Algorithm-level scope: what ISOSTER is not

ISOSTER is a non-parametric elliptical-isophote fitter for 2-D
images. Three classes of analysis are out of scope by design and
will remain so:

- **Parametric model fitting** — Sérsic, double-Sérsic, exponential
  + bulge, multi-component fits, GALFIT-style $\chi^2$ minimization
  over component parameters. ISOSTER's output is a non-parametric
  description of the isophotal structure; users who need
  parametric decomposition should pair ISOSTER with a dedicated
  tool (`GALFIT`, `imfit`, `pysersic`, `astropy.modeling`). The
  output schemas are not incompatible — ISOSTER's results
  dictionary contains enough information to seed a parametric
  fit at the initial-guess level — but the parametric fit itself
  is a separate analysis.

- **PSF deconvolution and PSF-aware fitting.** The harmonic fit
  operates on the observed image without modeling the PSF. The
  consequences are well-known: in the regime where the galaxy is
  small relative to the PSF FWHM, the fitted geometry is biased
  by the PSF convolution; in the regime where the galaxy is large
  but the PSF wings extend across many isophotes, the outer
  isophotes inherit a small wing-contribution bias. ISOSTER
  reports the geometry of the *observed* isophotes; converting to
  intrinsic geometries requires an external PSF model. For nearby
  galaxies with extended PSF wings (very nearby NGC objects, the
  inner halos of bright ellipticals, the wings of saturated stars
  near the target), this is the dominant scientific limitation.

- **3-D spectral cubes and multi-wavelength model fitting.** The
  multi-band joint free fit of §1.4.5 operates on co-aligned 2-D
  images, one per band. A spectroscopic IFU cube (MaNGA, SAMI,
  Mauve, the LSST 4MOST follow-up cubes) is *not* a 2-D image
  with extra bands; it is a $(x, y, \lambda)$ data structure
  whose isophote analysis would generalize the per-isophote loop
  along the wavelength axis. The single-band sampling stack
  generalizes naturally, but the corresponding driver, fitting
  loop, and output schema do not yet exist. IFU support is on the
  roadmap (§1.6.6) but is not in the current release.

A fourth out-of-scope class is *time-domain photometry* on
variable sources — supernova light-curve fitting, AGN
variability monitoring, transient followup. ISOSTER is not the
right tool for any of these.

## §1.6.2 Features whose defaults are still being calibrated

Several §1.4 features are in active use but with default
parameter values that we describe as *heuristic recommendations*
rather than survey-tested defaults. The list:

- **Outer-region center regularization (§1.4.3.1)** —
  `outer_reg_strength`, `outer_reg_sma_onset`, and
  `outer_reg_sma_width` were initially calibrated on HSC-SSP edge
  cases for deep-LSB disk-galaxy fits. The recommended ranges
  documented in the configuration reference apply to HSC-scale
  imaging; DECaLS / BASS / MzLS, with different seeing and sky
  brightness, may require different ranges. The form of the
  logistic ramp is robust to these differences (the function
  itself is well-behaved across surveys), but the recommended
  values are not yet survey-blind.

- **Automatic LSB lock trigger threshold (§1.4.3.2)** — the
  `lsb_auto_lock_maxgerr = 0.30` default was chosen to fire
  before the gradient-acceptability check at `maxgerr = 0.50`
  would have produced a stop code $-1$, with a debounce count of
  $2$. On bright galaxies in clean fields the lock essentially
  never fires (its purpose is to handle LSB regimes the
  bright-galaxy fit doesn't enter). On the deep LSB regime the
  defaults are tuned for HSC-scale imaging; the trigger threshold
  may need adjustment for surveys with substantially different
  per-pixel photon noise characteristics.

- **Multi-band loose-validity participation thresholds (§1.4.5.4)** —
  `loose_validity_min_per_band_count` and
  `loose_validity_min_per_band_frac` set the participation cuts
  below which a band is dropped from the joint fit on a given
  isophote. The defaults are lenient (a band is dropped only when
  it keeps fewer than 6 samples or under 20% of the rectangular
  sample count) but the
  optimal values depend on the typical per-band mask asymmetry of
  the input data. Surveys with consistently good per-band masking
  may want tighter cuts; surveys with
  patchy per-band masking may need to relax the cuts.

- **Lazy-gradient stall detector (§1.3.2)** — the three-iteration
  stall threshold at which the gradient is re-evaluated is a
  heuristic. It is set tight enough that the gradient is
  refreshed on essentially any case where the iteration is
  meaningfully slowing, and loose enough that ordinary
  convergence does not trigger a redundant re-evaluation. The
  default works well across the validated samples but is not
  individually configurable; users with pathological iteration
  trajectories may benefit from a future configurable threshold.

The calibration status is documented in
`docs/02-configuration-reference.md` for each of these fields. The
parameters are user-configurable, and the recommended values are
the *current* recommendations — they may be revised in subsequent
releases as the validation samples grow.

## §1.6.3 Mask and variance-map quality dependence

ISOSTER inherits a substantial amount of upstream-pipeline state
through its `mask` and `variance_map` arguments. The fit's
*algorithmic* correctness does not depend on these inputs; an
unmasked image with no variance map produces a correct
five-parameter harmonic fit by definition. The *quality* of the
fit on real survey data depends on them substantially.

**Mask quality.** A mask that misses bad pixels (defects, cosmic
rays, hot pixels, secondary sources) produces a harmonic fit
contaminated by those pixels. The variance-aware fit (§1.4.2)
provides automatic down-weighting of high-variance pixels, but it
does not provide masking — a survival weight of $10^{-6}$ from a
bad pixel with $\sigma^2 = 10^{6}$ is small but not zero, and a
band of bad pixels across an early isophote can swing the
harmonic amplitude by an amount that survives the WLS weighting.
The user is responsible for providing a mask that covers known
bad pixels; ISOSTER does not derive a mask from the image.

**Variance-map quality.** The WLS path of §1.4.2 weights each
sample by the user-supplied $1 / \sigma_i^2$. If the
variance map underestimates the true photon noise — typical of
coadded imaging where pixel-to-pixel noise is correlated and the
naive per-pixel variance does not reflect the effective noise per
resolution element — WLS produces over-confident error bars.
ISOSTER cannot diagnose this from the variance map alone; the
caller is responsible for upstream correction (a noise-rescaling
factor estimated from blank sky, a covariance-matrix model of the
coaddition). On survey data where the variance map is the
pipeline default, this is a known systematic that the benchmark
chapters discuss in detail.

**Background model quality.** The `sigma_bg` field (§1.4.4.6)
sets the photon-noise floor on the convergence criterion. A
poorly characterized background — under-subtracted sky, residual
gradients across the image, scattered light — biases the fit's
LSB-regime behaviour even when the variance map is correct.
ISOSTER provides no background subtraction; the user supplies the
sky-subtracted image and the documented `sigma_bg` value.

In all three cases the limitation is upstream from ISOSTER's
algorithm and ISOSTER cannot resolve it on its own. A survey
pipeline that consumes ISOSTER's output should therefore treat
mask quality, variance-map accuracy, and background subtraction
as *prerequisites* rather than as outputs of the ISOSTER fit.

## §1.6.4 Scattered light and extended-PSF regimes

A specific failure mode worth flagging separately is the regime
where the *extended PSF wings* of a bright source dominate the
outskirt isophotes of a faint nearby target. This includes:

- **Very nearby NGC galaxies** observed with HSC, DECam, or
  similar wide-field instruments where the PSF wings carry
  measurable flux out to $10$–$20\,\mathrm{arcmin}$.
- **The inner halos of bright ellipticals** in galaxy clusters,
  where the BCG's PSF wings contribute to the apparent isophote
  structure of nearby cluster members.
- **Saturated foreground stars** whose diffraction spikes
  intersect the target's outer isophotes; the binary mask removes
  the spike core but the wing structure is harder to characterize.

For these cases the LSB outskirt strategy of §1.4.3 reduces the
geometry drift but does not address the underlying scattered-light
contamination of the intensity profile. The fitted geometry under
the soft + hard composition may converge to a sensible value
while the intensity profile in the outer SMAs carries a
scattered-light bias that biases color gradients, mass-to-light
profile estimates, and intracluster-light measurements.

The proper resolution is upstream: a scattered-light model
appropriate to the instrument and exposure that is subtracted
from the image before ISOSTER is called. ISOSTER does not provide
such a model — there is no general-purpose scattered-light model
that works across instruments — and integrates with whichever
external scattered-light pipeline the survey provides.

Users analyzing nearby galaxies in crowded fields or BCGs in
cluster environments should consider this regime carefully when
interpreting outer-isophote intensities; the geometry may be
robust to the contamination while the intensity is not.

## §1.6.5 Computational considerations

Three computational considerations affect when ISOSTER is the
right tool to deploy.

**Numba cold-start cost.** The Numba-JIT inner kernels of §1.3.2
compile at first call, which costs roughly a second on a typical
modern machine. On a survey pipeline processing thousands of
galaxies the cost is amortized to negligibility; on a one-galaxy
analysis it is added to every cold-process run. The pure-NumPy
fallback (which the user can force through environment
configuration) avoids the JIT cost at the price of
approximately a factor of 3–5 slowdown on the hot kernels; for
a single-galaxy fit on a small image this is often net faster
than waiting for the JIT.

**Multi-band cost scaling.** The joint multi-band fit of §1.4.5
costs $\mathrm{O}(B)$ per iteration in the design-matrix
construction and the WLS row scaling. For typical survey-scale
$B \in \{3, 4, 5, 6\}$ the cost is a small multiple of the
single-band cost; for $B \in \{10, 20, \ldots\}$ (eventual
narrowband-survey applications: J-PAS, J-PLUS, PAU) the joint
fit's per-iteration cost becomes a significant pipeline item.
The configuration alternative for the high-$B$ regime is to use
the loose-validity path or to fit subsets of bands jointly with
forced photometry between subsets.

**Wall-time on large images.** The per-isophote cost is
asymptotically sublinear in image area through `map_coordinates`
interpolation (§1.3.3), so the dependence of fit time on image
size is mild. However, the *total* fit time depends on the SMA
range, which depends on image size when the target is well
resolved. A galaxy whose $a_\mathrm{max}$ is $400$ pixels in a
$2048 \times 2048$ image is roughly $4\times$ slower to fit than
the same galaxy at $a_\mathrm{max} = 200$ pixels — not because
the image is bigger but because the isophote count is.

The computational floor for ISOSTER is approximately a few hundred
milliseconds per galaxy on a typical image, dominated by the
SMA-grid walk rather than by any single isophote. On survey
samples in the thousands this works out to runtimes measured in
hours per pipeline pass, which is operationally comfortable. On
samples in the millions (LSST-scale photometric surveys) the
runtime becomes a multi-day item, which motivates the GPU
roadmap below.

## §1.6.6 Roadmap

The development directions we anticipate addressing in subsequent
releases, in approximate order of priority:

**GPU acceleration of hot kernels.** The §1.3.2 inner kernels —
design-matrix construction (single-band and joint multi-band),
ellipse-coordinate construction, harmonic-model evaluation — are
natural candidates
for GPU acceleration through CuPy, JAX, or a vectorized PyTorch
backend. The vectorized sampling step (`scipy.ndimage.map_coordinates`)
has analogous GPU implementations available. We anticipate a
$10\times$ wall-time reduction on the hot kernels alone, with
correspondingly larger reductions when the GPU implementation
fuses the kernel invocations across SMAs and across galaxies in a
batch.

The motivation for the GPU roadmap is *survey scale*. LSST-class
photometric surveys with $\mathrm{O}(10^6)$ resolved galaxies per
visit deliver more data than CPU-bound ISOSTER can comfortably
process in real time. The GPU path makes the per-galaxy cost low
enough that survey-pipeline integration is mechanical rather than
performance-engineering.

**Spectral / IFU cube support.** The per-isophote loop generalizes
naturally to a per-(isophote, wavelength) loop on a 3-D
$(x, y, \lambda)$ cube. The wavelength axis enters through the
sampling stack (sampling needs to interpolate along $\lambda$ in
addition to $(x, y)$) and through the output schema (the
results dictionary needs a wavelength dimension). The
single-band sampling stack already encapsulates the
implementation that the cube extension would build on, so the
extension is a generalization rather than a rewrite. We
anticipate IFU support landing in a paper-II release alongside
applications to MaNGA, SAMI, and Mauve cubes.

**Tighter PSF-aware modes.** A PSF-aware fitting mode would
deconvolve the PSF from the image before isophote sampling, or
equivalently convolve the harmonic-fit model with the PSF before
comparison to the observed isophote. The former requires
deconvolution machinery; the latter requires a forward-model
generalization of the harmonic decomposition. The forward-model
approach is more numerically stable and is the direction we
anticipate pursuing. Initial development targets the small-galaxy
regime where the PSF FWHM is a non-trivial fraction of the
galaxy's effective radius; the large-galaxy regime with extended
PSF wings remains an upstream-pipeline problem.

**Cross-tool QA comparison plumbing.** The AutoProf-coefficient-scale
porting gap mentioned in §1.4.6.2 and §1.5.3 is a known item in
the cross-tool comparison plumbing. Resolution requires either
porting AutoProf's higher-harmonic basis to the Bender-normalized
convention or providing an explicit conversion factor in the QA
plotting code. The latter is the planned approach; once
implemented, the §1.5 feature matrix's "(✓) different scale"
entries for AutoProf become full ✓.

**Survey-blind LSB defaults.** The §1.6.2 calibration items will
be tightened as the validation sample grows. The aim is a single
recommended default for each LSB-regime parameter that works
acceptably across HSC, DECaLS, BASS / MzLS, DES, and the
eventual LSST main survey, with documented deviations only for
genuinely exceptional surveys.

A handful of smaller items — better error reporting on
configuration mistakes, an interactive Jupyter QA panel,
finer-grained logging — are also on the roadmap but are
operational improvements rather than algorithmic ones; we do not
itemize them here.

## §1.6.7 Closing remarks on the technical chapter

The technical chapter began with a three-goal framing — speed,
flexibility, scientific power — and traversed §1.1 through §1.6
demonstrating how ISOSTER meets each. The algorithmic-speed
argument of §1.3 establishes the first goal at the level of
operation counts and code-path organization. The configurability
surface of §1.2 and the six feature subsections of §1.4
establish the second goal by exposing every algorithmic feature
as an orthogonal flag on a single validated configuration object.
The scientific-power argument runs through the same six §1.4
subsections, each of which demonstrates one new capability the
classical Python predecessors do not jointly provide:
eccentric-anomaly sampling and joint ISOFIT harmonic fitting
faithfully reproduced from Ciambur 2015; variance-aware
fitting with exact gradient-error propagation; a soft + hard
composition for the LSB outskirt regime; operations-grade batch
robustness primitives; joint multi-band free fitting that
supersedes forced photometry for color-gradient science; and a
QA / reproducibility infrastructure that ties the fit outputs to
the configuration that produced them through round-trip
serialization.

The aggregate is a tool calibrated for the next generation of
deep, wide-field, multi-band imaging surveys — HSC-SSP, the
late-stage DECaLS / BASS / MzLS samples, DES, the eventual LSST
main survey — and structured so that the surveys' principal
challenges (LSB outskirts, multi-band joint fitting, throughput
at sample scale, reproducibility across pipeline runs) are
directly addressed at the algorithmic level. The benchmark
chapters that follow this technical chapter quantify the actual
performance on the Huang2013, S4G, and SGA-2020 campaigns and
provide the empirical evidence behind the §1.3 and §1.5
qualitative claims.

The closing observation is that ISOSTER is *not finished* — the
§1.6 roadmap names several substantial development directions
that the next release cycles will address — but the version
documented in this chapter is *operational* in the sense that all
six §1.4 features ship with regression-test coverage, all
configurations route through the same Pydantic validation, and
all outputs round-trip through the three serialization paths
documented in §1.4.6.3. Users can deploy the current version on
their survey pipelines today; the roadmap items are improvements
to that operational baseline, not blockers to it.

The technical chapter ends here. The benchmark chapters that
follow apply the framework of §1.4.6.4 to the three campaign
datasets and report the quantitative cross-tool comparisons that
this chapter has carefully avoided.
