# §1.4.4 Robustness primitives for batch and survey pipelines

The features described in §1.4.1 through §1.4.3 are *algorithmic*
in the sense that they change what the fit computes. The features in
the present subsection are *operational*: they do not change the
algorithm itself but change how the algorithm reacts to data that
violates one or more implicit assumptions — a corrupted starting
geometry, a bad pixel in the middle of an early isophote, a galaxy
that lies near an image edge, an unusually shallow gradient at
$a_0$. None of these scenarios is rare in a survey-scale fit. On a
sample of thousands of galaxies, the per-galaxy probability of an
unrecoverable failure mode is what matters, and the fallback
behaviour when the anchor isophote fails — return the central-pixel
measurement and stop — leaves the affected galaxies with effectively
no fit at all. We have not measured the rate at which that happens on
a representative survey sample, and quote no figure for it; the
campaigns of the benchmark chapters are where such a number would
come from.

The robustness primitives in this section address that failure mode.
Each is independently a small change. The intent is to move the
remaining failures toward genuine data pathology
(catastrophic mask coverage, unmasked saturated companions, image
edges within $a_0$) rather than algorithmic fragility. Two of the
primitives are always on (gradient-SNR-aware damping and
per-iteration step clipping, §1.4.4.4–§1.4.4.5) and the
first-isophote retry is enabled with a small default budget; the
rest are opt-in so existing single-galaxy workflows are largely
unaffected. For survey pipelines the standard recommendation is to
enable all of them.

## §1.4.4.1 First-isophote retry

When the anchor isophote at $a_0$ fails to converge with an
acceptable stop code, the classical algorithm has no recourse. The
fit terminates after the central-pixel measurement, returning a
results dictionary with only one entry, and the survey pipeline
loses the galaxy. The anchor failure can have many causes — $a_0$
chosen too small relative to the galaxy structure, $a_0$ chosen
too large for the image size, the starting ellipticity or PA
incompatible with the actual isophote shape at $a_0$, a bad pixel
patch intersecting the trial ellipse — and many of them are
recoverable by perturbing the starting conditions.

ISOSTER exposes a configurable retry mechanism through the
`max_retry_first_isophote` field (default $3$; $0$ disables it).
When the anchor fails, ISOSTER catches the failure
and re-attempts the fit up to $K$ additional times with perturbed
starting conditions. The perturbation cycle through
$(a_0, \varepsilon_0, \mathrm{PA}_0)$ space is deterministic: the
first five attempts follow the fixed schedule
$(0.8\,a_0, \varepsilon, \mathrm{PA})$,
$(1.3\,a_0, \varepsilon, \mathrm{PA})$,
$(0.6\,a_0, 0.05, \mathrm{PA})$,
$(1.5\,a_0, \varepsilon, \mathrm{PA} + \pi/4)$,
$(0.5\,a_0, 0.05, \mathrm{PA} + \pi/2)$
— that is, smaller and larger $a_0$ first, then progressively
near-circular ellipticity and rotated PA. Beyond five attempts an
extended cycle continues with $a_0$ factors $(0.4, 0.7, 1.1, 1.6,
2.0)$, alternating the near-circular $\varepsilon = 0.05$ and
stepping PA by $\pi/6$ per attempt. The schedule is designed to
explore both larger and smaller
$a_0$, both higher and lower ellipticity, and both rotation
directions so that an anchor failure caused by any one bad guess
is likely to be cured by one of the perturbations.

Two output fields document the retry trail. The boolean
`first_isophote_failure` is set on the top-level results dictionary
only when the anchor *and* every retry and probe attempt failed —
a successful retry leaves it unset. The list
`first_isophote_retry_log` is
populated only when retries actually ran; each entry is a small
dictionary recording the attempted $(a_0, \varepsilon_0,
\mathrm{PA}_0)$ and the resulting stop code. The log is useful for
post-mortem analysis on the rare galaxy where even the retry
mechanism fails: it indicates which corner of the perturbation
space the algorithm explored before giving up.

A companion field, `first_isophote_fail_count` (default $3$),
controls a last-resort probe. If the anchor and all retries failed,
ISOSTER probes the next
$N - 1$ growth steps outward with the original starting geometry;
if one of them converges it becomes the anchor. Only when the
anchor *and* those probes all fail does ISOSTER emit a
`FIRST_FEW_ISOPHOTE_FAILURE` warning and set
`first_isophote_failure`. The probe loop is never entered when the
anchor itself converged, so the warning cannot fire on a fit whose
anchor succeeded.

A reasonable batch setting is `max_retry_first_isophote = 5`. It
reaches the near-circular and rotated-PA corners of the schedule,
which are the ones that address a bad initial-geometry guess, while
bounding the worst-case wall time per galaxy at a small multiple of
the typical single-fit cost. What fraction of anchor failures it
actually recovers has not been measured, and the demonstration that
would measure it — a panel of failure-mode SGA-2020 galaxies — has not
been performed. Two bounds on the
schedule are worth knowing. Each perturbed $a_0$ is clamped to at
least one pixel and each perturbed ellipticity to $[0, 0.99]$, so an
aggressive factor cannot generate a degenerate trial geometry. And
the schedule is finite: it contains ten entries, five fixed plus
five extended, so although the config field accepts values up to
$20$, anything above $10$ requests attempts that do not exist and
yields ten. There is no randomised continuation beyond the
schedule — the retry sequence is fully deterministic, which is what
makes a failed galaxy's `first_isophote_retry_log` reproducible.

## §1.4.4.2 Permissive geometry propagation

The classical algorithm propagates the *converged* geometry of
isophote $k$ as the starting geometry of isophote $k + 1$. When
isophote $k$ fails to converge — gradient failure, too few sample
points, sigma-clipping anomaly — the algorithm has no propagated
geometry to use, and isophote $k + 1$ has to be initialized from
either the original anchor geometry or a fallback. In either case
the propagation chain is broken at isophote $k$, and a
single-isophote failure can cascade: $k + 1$ has worse starting
conditions than it would have had, fails more easily, and the
failure can poison $k + 2$ in turn.

The `permissive_geometry = True` flag (default `False`) changes the
propagation rule to use the *latest fitted geometry* even when the
fit was not acceptable. The fit-loop bookkeeping that tracks the
best-so-far geometry for the stop-code-$2$ fallback (§1.1) is
repurposed here: if isophote $k$'s converged result is
unacceptable, the propagated geometry for isophote $k + 1$ is the
best-iteration geometry from $k$'s fit attempt, with its associated
$|a_\max|$ and stop code carried separately. The next isophote
therefore starts from the closest-to-converged geometry the
previous attempt reached, which is usually much better than the
anchor geometry $k - j$ steps removed.

The flag's name reflects the policy: ISOSTER becomes permissive
about which iteration's geometry counts as "propagated". The
classical strict policy ("only propagate from accepted stop codes")
is preserved as the default for the same backwards-compatibility
reason as the rest of §1.4.4. For survey pipelines the cost of the
permissive policy is negligible — there is no extra computation,
only a different selection of which geometry to carry forward —
and the gain is a substantially shorter failure cascade.

## §1.4.4.3 Adaptive integration

The `integrator` config field selects the estimator used to reduce a
ring of samples to a single number. Its most visible effect is on
the intensity reported in the output dictionary's `intens` field,
but its scope is wider than that and worth stating up front: the
same choice is passed to the gradient computation, so it also
governs how the two ring statistics whose difference forms
$dI/da$ are located, and therefore which variance formula their
uncertainty uses (§1.4.2.2). Switching to the median changes the
gradient, and through the gradient the geometry updates and the
`maxgerr` gate — not only a reported column.
The classical algorithm uses the mean of the harmonic-fit intercept
$I_0$. The mean is the maximum-likelihood estimate of the average
intensity along the ellipse under Gaussian noise; it is the right
choice in the high-$S/N$ regime where the residual distribution is
approximately Gaussian.

In the LSB regime the residual distribution is often *not*
Gaussian. Faint companions, scattered light, and small unmasked
defects produce asymmetric tails in the per-sample intensity
distribution; the mean is sensitive to those tails while the
median is not. The classical fix is to switch the integrator to
`'median'` globally, which is correct in the LSB regime but loses
a small amount of precision in the inner region where the mean is
unbiased.

The `'adaptive'` integrator combines the two. When the user supplies
`integrator='adaptive'` together with a transition SMA in the
`lsb_sma_threshold` field, ISOSTER uses the harmonic-fit mean for
isophotes with $a \le a_\mathrm{thresh}$ and the median for
isophotes with $a > a_\mathrm{thresh}$. The cross-parameter
validator rejects `integrator='adaptive'` without
`lsb_sma_threshold`, forcing the user to make a deliberate choice
of where the transition should be.

A natural pairing is `integrator='adaptive'` with
`lsb_sma_threshold` placed where the profile actually enters the
noise-dominated regime for the galaxy and imaging at hand — for
instance where the surface brightness approaches the sky level, or
where the gradient SNR falls toward the `maxgerr` gate. A fraction
of $a_\mathrm{max}$ is a convenient stand-in but not a
recommendation: $a_\mathrm{max}$ is a user-chosen fitting limit, not
a property of the galaxy, so tying the integrator transition to it
couples an estimator choice to an unrelated setting. The automatic LSB lock (§1.4.3) also
takes a per-axis integrator switch through `lsb_auto_lock_integrator`
(default `'median'`); when both `'adaptive'` integration and the
auto-lock are active, the locked clone overrides the adaptive
integrator with its own setting for the locked tail.

## §1.4.4.4 Gradient-SNR-aware damping

The per-iteration geometry update is multiplied by a damping
factor `geometry_damping` (default $0.7$) before being applied,
which limits the per-step displacement and prevents the iteration
from overshooting the converged geometry. This is a static damping:
the same factor is used on every iteration of every isophote.

In the LSB regime the radial gradient $dI/da$ that enters the
geometry-update conversion (§1.1) has a small absolute value and a
large fractional uncertainty $\sigma_{dI/da} / |dI/da|$. The
geometry-update step is proportional to $|a_\max| / |dI/da|$, so
small fractional gradient errors translate to large absolute
geometry corrections in that regime. The static damping factor
$0.7$ provides only a small safety margin against this dynamic
range expansion.

ISOSTER applies an additional *gradient-SNR-aware* damping factor
when the local gradient SNR

$$
\mathrm{SNR}_\mathrm{grad}
= \frac{\left|\widehat{dI/da}\right|}{\sigma_{dI/da}}
$$

falls below a threshold (internally set to $3$). The geometry-update
damping is then multiplied by an additional factor
$\mathrm{clip}(\mathrm{SNR}_\mathrm{grad} / 3,\ 0.1,\ 1)$ — linear
in the gradient SNR with a floor of $0.1$ — so that the
iteration step shrinks in proportion to the gradient
informativeness without ever vanishing entirely. The mechanism is
purely defensive: it does not
prevent the iteration from converging in the high-$S/N$ regime
(where $\mathrm{SNR}_\mathrm{grad} \gg 3$ and the additional factor
is essentially $1$), and it bounds the iteration step in the
low-$S/N$ regime by an amount that matches the noise level of the
gradient itself.

There is no config flag to disable the mechanism, because the
alternative — uniform damping regardless of gradient informativeness
— is strictly worse in the LSB regime and exactly equivalent in the
high-$S/N$ regime.

It is not, however, unconditional, and the condition deserves
naming because it bites in exactly the regime the mechanism targets.
The extra damping is applied only when a gradient *uncertainty* is
available; when $\sigma_{dI/da}$ is unavailable the static
`geometry_damping` alone is used. Unavailability is not a rare
edge case. ISOSTER reports the gradient error as unavailable
whenever a ring's variance is non-finite, and — more importantly —
whenever the gradient estimate itself has been rejected as anomalous
and replaced by a fraction of the previous isophote's value. That
substitution is a real gradient measurement being discarded, so
declining to attach an uncertainty to the surrogate is the honest
choice; but it means the SNR-aware damping switches off at precisely
the isophotes whose gradients were bad enough to need replacing. The
hard step caps of §1.4.4.5 are the defence that remains active
there, which is part of why they are on by default rather than
opt-in.

## §1.4.4.5 Per-iteration step clipping

A complementary defence against runaway iteration steps operates
through *hard caps* on the per-iteration geometry change. Three
configuration fields set these caps:

- `clip_max_shift` (default $5.0\,\mathrm{px}$): maximum allowed
  centre shift per iteration.
- `clip_max_pa` (default $0.5\,\mathrm{rad} \approx 29^\circ$):
  maximum allowed PA change per iteration.
- `clip_max_eps` (default $0.1$): maximum allowed ellipticity
  change per iteration.

After the gradient-SNR-aware damping has been applied, each
proposed update is clipped to lie within the corresponding hard
cap. The clip is *direction-preserving*: a proposed shift of
$(8\,\mathrm{px}, 0)$ becomes $(5\,\mathrm{px}, 0)$ rather than
$(5\,\mathrm{px}, 5\,\mathrm{px})$, so the iteration moves in the
direction the harmonic decomposition prescribes but no further than
the cap permits per step.

The centre cap is not a flat five pixels in both update modes. Under
the default `'largest'` mode, which moves one coordinate per
iteration, the scalar displacement is clipped at `clip_max_shift`
directly. Under `geometry_update_mode='simultaneous'`, which moves
both centre axes at once, the *vector* shift is rescaled to a length
of at most $\max(\mathtt{clip\_max\_shift},\ 0.05\,a)$ — a
radius-scaled floor. The reason is that a fixed five-pixel cap is a
severe constraint on an isophote at $a = 300\,\mathrm{px}$, where it
amounts to $1.7\%$ of the semi-major axis and can effectively freeze
the outer geometry; scaling the floor with radius keeps the cap
meaningful as a *runaway* guard at all radii instead of becoming a
de facto geometry lock in the outskirts. The two modes therefore
apply the same field with different semantics, which is worth
knowing before comparing their outputs.

The defaults are conservative — wide enough that the cap is
inactive in normal fits, but tight enough to bound the worst-case
single-iteration excursion. The mechanism's purpose is not to
constrain the converged geometry (which is determined by the
iteration as a whole, not by any single step) but to prevent
catastrophic single-iteration excursions in pathological inputs:
a defect band crossing the trial ellipse can produce an enormous
spurious harmonic amplitude, which without clipping would be
converted by the small-gradient denominator into an enormous
geometry update, which would then cause the next iteration to
sample a completely different region of the image and produce
further pathological behaviour. The clip caps stop the cascade at
its first step.

Each field accepts `None` to disable that particular cap. The
classical algorithm corresponds to all three set to `None`; the
default ISOSTER values are the conservative caps described above.
The `'simultaneous'` value of `geometry_update_mode` pairs naturally
with somewhat smaller caps because all four parameters are updated
each iteration; the `'largest'` default mode only updates one
parameter per iteration and so the default caps are well-suited.

## §1.4.4.6 Background-noise convergence floor

The convergence criterion of §1.1, $|a_\max| < c \cdot S(a) \cdot
\sigma_\mathrm{eff}$ (with $S(a)$ the convergence-scale factor of
§1.1), uses the effective standard deviation
$\sigma_\mathrm{eff} = \max(\sigma_\mathrm{res}, \sigma_\mathrm{bg} / \sqrt{N})$
where $\sigma_\mathrm{res}$ is the harmonic-fit residual RMS and
$\sigma_\mathrm{bg}$ is the user-supplied background noise level.
The background floor is delivered through the `sigma_bg` config
field (default `None`, i.e. disabled, so $\sigma_\mathrm{eff} =
\sigma_\mathrm{res}$).

When `sigma_bg` is supplied, ISOSTER uses
$\sigma_\mathrm{bg} / \sqrt{N}$ as a lower bound on
$\sigma_\mathrm{eff}$, with $N$ the number of surviving sample
points along the isophote after masking and sigma clipping. The
The motivation for the floor is that $\sigma_\mathrm{bg}/\sqrt{N}$
is the scale on which the mean intensity of a ring of $N$ samples
can be determined at all, so a convergence threshold that shrinks
far below it is asking the fit to resolve structure the data cannot
carry. Left unfloored, $\sigma_\mathrm{res}$ can fall arbitrarily
low — a five-parameter model fitted to a noisy ring will sometimes
absorb a chance realisation closely — and the iteration then chases
noise-induced asymmetries indefinitely.

It is worth being careful about how strong a statement this is. The
floor is an *operational heuristic*, not a proof that a smaller
residual is physically impossible. Nothing forbids a particular
ring's post-fit residual from landing below the background noise
scale: $\sigma_\mathrm{res}$ is an estimate from a finite sample and
fluctuates, and the five fitted parameters remove real degrees of
freedom from it. What the floor asserts is only that ISOSTER should
not *act* on such a value by tightening its convergence demand,
because doing so trades a real risk (overfitting noise) against no
real gain. Read as a statement about the algorithm's behaviour it is
sound; read as a statement about the data it would be wrong.

When a variance map is supplied (§1.4.2), the WLS intensity error
already encodes the per-pixel photon noise directly, and the
`sigma_bg` field is typically left at its
default. The `sigma_bg` parameter is most useful in the
no-variance-map OLS workflow on imaging where the sky noise is
well-characterized externally (typical of surveys with documented
sky-brightness models). The value to use is the sky noise of the
image being fitted, in the image's own intensity units, and it should
be measured — from blank-sky regions of the frame, or from the
survey's own noise model — rather than taken from a table. We do not
quote a per-survey default, because the correct value depends on
coadd depth, the units the caller's image is in, and the sky
subtraction already applied.

## §1.4.4.7 Composition and defaults

Each of the six primitives is independently configurable. Most
defaults reproduce the classical algorithm, with two deliberate
exceptions noted above: the first-isophote retry is enabled with a
budget of three attempts, and gradient-SNR damping and step
clipping are always on (their floors are loose enough to be
inactive in normal fits). For most science use cases on small
samples — a single
target galaxy, a small panel of nearby objects — the defaults are
the right choice and none of the primitives needs to be touched.

For survey pipelines, a reasonable starting point enables the
primitives whose cost is bounded and whose failure mode is benign.
This is a suggested configuration, not a named preset: ISOSTER
ships no `survey` profile, and the values below are a starting point
to be tuned against a pilot sample rather than defaults validated
across surveys. In particular `lsb_sma_threshold` is
galaxy-and-survey dependent and the value shown is illustrative
only.

```python
config = IsosterConfig(
    sma0=10.0, maxsma=200.0,
    # Operations-grade robustness
    max_retry_first_isophote=5,
    permissive_geometry=True,
    integrator="adaptive",
    lsb_sma_threshold=80.0,   # illustrative; tune per survey
)
```

The two primitives that are *always on* — gradient-SNR-aware
damping and per-iteration step clipping — do not appear in the
preset because their conservative defaults are already appropriate;
they would only need to be tightened on a pipeline where step
clipping is biting genuine large-step iterations (rare).

The composability story for the robustness primitives is identical
to that of every other ISOSTER feature: each primitive operates on
a different layer of the fit (anchor retry at the image level,
permissive geometry at the inter-isophote propagation level,
adaptive integration at the per-isophote output, gradient-SNR
damping at the per-iteration update, step clipping at the
per-iteration cap, $\sigma_\mathrm{bg}$ at the convergence test),
and none of them shares mutable state with any other. Enabling all
of them simultaneously is the suggested survey-pipeline starting
point above; selectively enabling a subset (e.g. only
`max_retry_first_isophote` on a small panel where the user happens
to be running into anchor failures) is also fully supported.
