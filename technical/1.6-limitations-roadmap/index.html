    §1.6 Limitations and roadmap

    The technical chapter closes with an honest accounting of where ISOSTER currently falls short and where the development effort is heading. The limitations described below are not failures relative to a putative ideal tool; they are the boundaries of what ISOSTER covers in the version this paper documents. Some are intrinsic to the algorithmic scope (2-D images, non-parametric isophote analysis); some are operational (compute cost on cold starts, calibration of LSB-regime defaults); some are deferred (GPU kernels, IFU cube support). The roadmap items at the end of the subsection name the development directions we anticipate addressing in subsequent releases, without committing to a release schedule.

    The honest-accounting framing matters for the same reason the per-feature demonstration sections in §1.4 were cherry-picked but honest: a tool that overstates its scope produces application failures downstream, and a tool that understates its scope is under-used. The aim of this subsection is to leave the reader with an accurate map of what ISOSTER will and will not do on a given problem.

    §1.6.1 Algorithm-level scope: what ISOSTER is not

    ISOSTER is a non-parametric elliptical-isophote fitter for 2-D images. Three classes of analysis are out of scope by design and will remain so:

    • Parametric model fitting — Sérsic, double-Sérsic, exponential

    • bulge, multi-component fits, GALFIT-style \(\chi^2\) minimization over component parameters. ISOSTER's output is a non-parametric description of the isophotal structure; users who need parametric decomposition should pair ISOSTER with a dedicated tool (GALFIT, imfit, pysersic, astropy.modeling). The output schemas are not incompatible — ISOSTER's results dictionary contains enough information to seed a parametric fit at the initial-guess level — but the parametric fit itself is a separate analysis.

    • PSF deconvolution and PSF-aware fitting. The harmonic fit operates on the observed image without modeling the PSF. The consequences are well-known: in the regime where the galaxy is small relative to the PSF FWHM, the fitted geometry is biased by the PSF convolution; in the regime where the galaxy is large but the PSF wings extend across many isophotes, the outer isophotes inherit a small wing-contribution bias. ISOSTER reports the geometry of the observed isophotes; converting to intrinsic geometries requires an external PSF model. For nearby galaxies with extended PSF wings (very nearby NGC objects, the inner halos of bright ellipticals, the wings of saturated stars near the target), this is the dominant scientific limitation.

    • 3-D spectral cubes and multi-wavelength model fitting. The multi-band joint free fit of §1.4.5 operates on co-aligned 2-D images, one per band. A spectroscopic IFU cube (MaNGA, SAMI, Mauve, the LSST 4MOST follow-up cubes) is not a 2-D image with extra bands; it is a \((x, y, \lambda)\) data structure whose isophote analysis would generalize the per-isophote loop along the wavelength axis. The single-band sampling stack generalizes naturally, but the corresponding driver, fitting loop, and output schema do not yet exist. IFU support is on the roadmap (§1.6.6) but is not in the current release.

    A fourth out-of-scope class is time-domain photometry on variable sources — supernova light-curve fitting, AGN variability monitoring, transient followup. ISOSTER is not the right tool for any of these.

    §1.6.2 Features whose defaults are still being calibrated

    Several §1.4 features are in active use but with default parameter values that we describe as heuristic recommendations rather than survey-tested defaults. The list:

    • Outer-region center regularization (§1.4.3.1) — outer_reg_strength, outer_reg_sma_onset, and outer_reg_sma_width were initially calibrated on HSC-SSP edge cases for deep-LSB disk-galaxy fits. The recommended ranges documented in the configuration reference apply to HSC-scale imaging; DECaLS / BASS / MzLS, with different seeing and sky brightness, may require different ranges. The form of the logistic ramp is robust to these differences (the function itself is well-behaved across surveys), but the recommended values are not yet survey-blind.

    • Automatic LSB lock trigger threshold (§1.4.3.2) — the lsb_auto_lock_maxgerr = 0.30 default was chosen to fire before the gradient-acceptability check at maxgerr = 0.50 would have produced a stop code \(-1\), with a debounce count of \(2\). On bright galaxies in clean fields the lock essentially never fires (its purpose is to handle LSB regimes the bright-galaxy fit doesn't enter). On the deep LSB regime the defaults are tuned for HSC-scale imaging; the trigger threshold may need adjustment for surveys with substantially different per-pixel photon noise characteristics.

    • Multi-band loose-validity participation thresholds (§1.4.5.4) — loose_validity_min_per_band_count and loose_validity_min_per_band_frac set the participation cuts below which a band is dropped from the joint fit on a given isophote. The defaults are lenient (a band is dropped only when it keeps fewer than 6 samples or under 20% of the rectangular sample count) but the optimal values depend on the typical per-band mask asymmetry of the input data. Surveys with consistently good per-band masking may want tighter cuts; surveys with patchy per-band masking may need to relax the cuts.

    • Lazy-gradient stall detector (§1.3.2) — the three-iteration stall threshold at which the gradient is re-evaluated is a heuristic. It is set tight enough that the gradient is refreshed on essentially any case where the iteration is meaningfully slowing, and loose enough that ordinary convergence does not trigger a redundant re-evaluation. The default works well across the validated samples but is not individually configurable; users with pathological iteration trajectories may benefit from a future configurable threshold.

    This entry is a calibration item, but the underlying feature is also a genuine accuracy limitation and belongs on the list as such. As §1.3.2 quantifies, the lazy gradient is a fast approximation, not an exact reformulation: converged geometries differ from the use_lazy_gradient=False reference by a median of well under one percent of the fitted uncertainty, but with a tail that grows as the data get noisier — reaching \(2.6\sigma\) on the worst isophote of a \(S/N = 10\) test sequence. The regime where the approximation is loosest is the LSB outskirt regime of §1.4.3, which is also where several of ISOSTER's science cases live. Users measuring per-isophote geometry in that regime should consider paying the roughly 31% surcharge for the exact path. A better resolution — refreshing the gradient adaptively on a gradient-SNR criterion rather than on a fixed stall count, so the approximation tightens automatically where it is weakest — is on the roadmap.

    • Outer-region regularization does not compose with ISOFIT (§1.4.3.3) — the Tikhonov shrinkage is applied where a five-parameter harmonic solve becomes a geometry step, and was never wired into the wider ISOFIT joint solver. Requesting use_outer_center_regularization=True together with simultaneous_harmonics=True therefore retains only the selector-level penalty and silently loses the per-iteration step damping. ISOSTER warns at configuration time, so the gap is announced rather than hidden, but it is the one place in §1.4 where two features do not give the union of their effects. Closing it means threading the per-axis Jacobian through the wider solve; it is a bounded piece of work that has simply not been done.

    • Per-axis regularization weights are effectively binary (§1.4.3.1) — measured on a BCG test case, the Tikhonov blend factor saturates above \(99.99\%\) for any per-axis weight in the range a user would plausibly type, because the harmonic-to-geometry Jacobian grows without bound as the radial gradient approaches zero. Intermediate weights such as \(\{1, 0.5, 0.5\}\) therefore behave like \(\{1, 1, 1\}\), and the honest "partial damping" regime would require weights around \(10^{-4}\). The field is documented as a binary switch, but the fact that a continuous-looking parameter is not in fact continuous over its useful range is a design wart rather than a feature.

    The calibration status is documented in docs/02-configuration-reference.md for each of these fields. The parameters are user-configurable, and the recommended values are the current recommendations — they may be revised in subsequent releases as the validation samples grow.

    §1.6.3 Mask and variance-map quality dependence

    ISOSTER inherits a substantial amount of upstream-pipeline state through its mask and variance_map arguments. The fit's algorithmic correctness does not depend on these inputs; an unmasked image with no variance map produces a correct five-parameter harmonic fit by definition. The quality of the fit on real survey data depends on them substantially.

    Mask quality. A mask that misses bad pixels (defects, cosmic rays, hot pixels, secondary sources) produces a harmonic fit contaminated by those pixels. The variance-aware fit (§1.4.2) provides automatic down-weighting of high-variance pixels, but it does not provide masking — a survival weight of \(10^{-6}\) from a bad pixel with \(\sigma^2 = 10^{6}\) is small but not zero, and a band of bad pixels across an early isophote can swing the harmonic amplitude by an amount that survives the WLS weighting. The user is responsible for providing a mask that covers known bad pixels; ISOSTER does not derive a mask from the image.

    Variance-map quality. The WLS path of §1.4.2 weights each sample by the user-supplied \(1 / \sigma_i^2\). If the variance map underestimates the true photon noise — typical of coadded imaging where pixel-to-pixel noise is correlated and the naive per-pixel variance does not reflect the effective noise per resolution element — WLS produces over-confident error bars. ISOSTER cannot diagnose this from the variance map alone; the caller is responsible for upstream correction (a noise-rescaling factor estimated from blank sky, a covariance-matrix model of the coaddition). On survey data where the variance map is the pipeline default, this is a known systematic that the benchmark chapters discuss in detail.

    Background model quality. The sigma_bg field (§1.4.4.6) sets the photon-noise floor on the convergence criterion. A poorly characterized background — under-subtracted sky, residual gradients across the image, scattered light — biases the fit's LSB-regime behaviour even when the variance map is correct. ISOSTER provides no background subtraction; the user supplies the sky-subtracted image and the documented sigma_bg value.

    In all three cases the limitation is upstream from ISOSTER's algorithm and ISOSTER cannot resolve it on its own. A survey pipeline that consumes ISOSTER's output should therefore treat mask quality, variance-map accuracy, and background subtraction as prerequisites rather than as outputs of the ISOSTER fit.

    §1.6.4 Scattered light and extended-PSF regimes

    A specific failure mode worth flagging separately is the regime where the extended PSF wings of a bright source dominate the outskirt isophotes of a faint nearby target. This includes:

    • Very nearby NGC galaxies observed with HSC, DECam, or similar wide-field instruments where the PSF wings carry measurable flux out to \(10\)–\(20\,\mathrm{arcmin}\).
    • The inner halos of bright ellipticals in galaxy clusters, where the BCG's PSF wings contribute to the apparent isophote structure of nearby cluster members.
    • Saturated foreground stars whose diffraction spikes intersect the target's outer isophotes; the binary mask removes the spike core but the wing structure is harder to characterize.

    For these cases the LSB outskirt strategy of §1.4.3 reduces the geometry drift but does not address the underlying scattered-light contamination of the intensity profile. The fitted geometry under the soft + hard composition may converge to a sensible value while the intensity profile in the outer SMAs carries a scattered-light bias that biases color gradients, mass-to-light profile estimates, and intracluster-light measurements.

    The proper resolution is upstream: a scattered-light model appropriate to the instrument and exposure that is subtracted from the image before ISOSTER is called. ISOSTER does not provide such a model — there is no general-purpose scattered-light model that works across instruments — and integrates with whichever external scattered-light pipeline the survey provides.

    Users analyzing nearby galaxies in crowded fields or BCGs in cluster environments should consider this regime carefully when interpreting outer-isophote intensities; the geometry may be robust to the contamination while the intensity is not.

    §1.6.5 Computational considerations

    Three computational considerations affect when ISOSTER is the right tool to deploy.

    Cold-start cost. Three separate costs are routinely conflated as "the JIT cost", and they call for different responses. Measured on the reference machine: importing the stack (Numba included) costs \(0.84\,\mathrm{s}\) (IQR \(0.83\)–\(0.86\)); the first fit against a populated Numba cache costs \(0.101\,\mathrm{s}\) (IQR \(0.099\)–\(0.103\)) more than a subsequent fit, which is the cache being read from disk rather than anything being compiled; and the first fit against an empty cache costs a further \(0.36\,\mathrm{s}\) (IQR \(0.36\)–\(0.37\)), which is the genuine compilation. Each is an across-session median over the archived run's empty-cache / populated-cache pairs — the measurement cannot be batched, since every observation costs an interpreter start — quoted to the precision its own spread supports. What matters is the ordering and the proportions.

    The compilation cost is paid once per compatible cache, not once per installation. Upgrading Numba or ISOSTER invalidates the cached objects, and an environment that cannot write the cache directory pays it on every process. The everyday cold-start penalty a user meets — a fresh process on a machine whose cache is already populated — is the import plus the cache load, a little under a second in total, of which none is compilation. On a survey pipeline processing thousands of galaxies the cost is amortized to negligibility; on a one-galaxy analysis it is added to every cold-process run.

    The pure-NumPy fallback (forced with NUMBA_DISABLE_JIT=1) avoids the compilation cost at the price of a slowdown on the hot kernels, so which is cheaper depends on how many fits a process performs. On the same small fixture as above the fallback is about \(1.23\times\) slower per fit in steady state. What it avoids is the compiled path's whole excess over its own steady state on a cache-less run — the compilation and the cache load together, not the compilation term alone, since a fallback process performs neither — and dividing the two puts the break-even at about 50 fits per process (IQR 47–52 across the archived sessions). A user fitting one galaxy in a fresh process is therefore genuinely better off with the fallback, and a pipeline fitting hundreds is not — the recommendation this paragraph used to make on intuition, now with the crossing point attached.

    Two things bound that number. The quartiles are tight but the upper tail is not: sessions ranged from 45 to 76 fits, so the crossing point is a few tens of fits rather than precisely fifty. And it is one fixture on one machine, with both terms sensitive to the workload — the per-fit penalty depends on what fraction of a fit is spent inside the compiled kernels, which varies with image size and ring count, and the start-up cost varies with how much of the library a given configuration touches. The ordering of the recommendation is robust; the threshold should be re-measured on any workload it is being applied to.

    Multi-band cost scaling. The joint multi-band fit of §1.4.5 is not linear in the number of bands, and the earlier claim that it costs \(\mathrm{O}(B)\) per iteration was wrong. The design-matrix construction and the WLS row scaling are indeed \(\mathrm{O}(BN)\), but the solve forms dense normal equations on a \((BN) \times (B+4)\) matrix, whose cost carries additional factors of \(B\). Measured on one joint solve with \(N = 700\) samples per band, for the geometry-parameterised solver that is the supported default (§1.4.5.2):

    \(B\) OLS ratio to previous WLS ratio to previous
    3 0.196 ms — 0.158 ms —
    6 0.333 ms 1.7× 0.351 ms 2.2×
    12 0.605 ms 1.8× 0.663 ms 1.9×
    24 2.49 ms 4.1× 2.60 ms 3.9×

    The scaling is close to linear through \(B \approx 12\) and departs beyond it. Three caveats on reading this. First, the final ratio is the least stable number in this chapter: over eighteen sessions its median is \(4.4\) with an interquartile range of \(4.2\)–\(4.5\), and it is the one aggregate whose median still shifts appreciably between independent sets. It supports "several times, not twice" and no finer statement. Second, the departure is milder for the supported geometry form than for the legacy shared-amplitude form, which the harness also times: the latter runs about \(6.0\times\) from \(B = 12\) to \(24\). Benchmarking the amplitude form and presenting it as the multi-band cost — as an earlier version of this section did — overstates the high-\(B\) penalty, because after an isophote's first iteration the default takes the geometry path. Third, we report the departure but do not diagnose it: attributing it to the cubic term in the normal-equations solve, to memory-hierarchy effects, or to a threading threshold in the host BLAS would require profiling we have not done. The \(B = 24\) entry is the least stable of the four across runs and machines, so read it as "several times, not twice". For typical survey-scale \(B \in \{3, 4, 5, 6\}\) the cost is therefore a small multiple of the single-band cost, as the linear intuition suggests; for \(B \in \{10, 20, \ldots\}\) (eventual narrowband-survey applications: J-PAS, J-PLUS, PAU) it becomes a significant pipeline item faster than a linear extrapolation would predict. The configuration alternative for the high-\(B\) regime is to fit reduced band subsets jointly, with forced photometry between subsets. Loose validity is not an alternative here, and an earlier version of this section suggested it was: the jagged path solves a \((\sum_b N_b) \times (B + 4)\) system, so it leaves the \(B\)-dependent column count untouched and commonly carries more rows than shared validity, since it stops discarding samples that only one band failed. It is a masking-policy choice with its own merits (§1.4.5.4), not a performance lever.

    Wall-time on large images. The per-isophote cost is asymptotically sublinear in image area through map_coordinates interpolation (§1.3.3), so the dependence of fit time on image size is mild. However, the total fit time depends on the SMA range, which depends on image size when the target is well resolved — but two quantities scale differently here and conflating them is what produced the \(4\times\) claim in an earlier version of this section. Under the default geometric SMA stepping the ring count grows only logarithmically: doubling \(a_\mathrm{max}\) adds \(\log 2 / \log(1 + \mathtt{astep})\) rings, about seven at the default astep = 0.1. But each ring carries \(N = \max(64, \lfloor 2\pi a \rfloor)\) samples, so the aggregate sampling work still grows roughly linearly in \(a_\mathrm{max}\). Measured on a galaxy chosen so that every ring converges out to 400 px — which matters more than it sounds, and is discussed below the table:

    \(a_\mathrm{max}\) rings sampling calls points sampled wall time
    100 px 56 460 66 600 47.4 ms
    200 px 63 516 118 886 58.8 ms
    400 px 70 574 225 744 78.4 ms

    All rings return stop code 0 at all three limits, with a median of seven iterations, so the three fits are doing comparable work per ring and the comparison is about the ring sequence rather than about convergence behaviour. The sampling columns are instrumented — counted from the actual calls, which include every iteration and the extra passes for gradient evaluation — not inferred from one nominal pass per output ring, which undercounts by roughly an order of magnitude.

    Two trends, stated separately because the measurement does not connect them. Ring count grows logarithmically (56 → 63 → 70), as geometric stepping requires. Points sampled grows almost exactly linearly (factors of 1.79 then 1.90 per doubling). Wall time lands between the two, rising by about \(1.3\times\) per doubling.

    Two things this does not establish. The first is a general scaling law for the wall-time growth. Over eighteen sessions the two step ratios are \(1.25\) (IQR \(1.23\)–\(1.27\)) for \(100 \to 200\,\mathrm{px}\) and \(1.32\) (IQR \(1.30\)–\(1.34\)) for \(200 \to 400\,\mathrm{px}\). The gap between them is real on this fixture, as the paired comparison below shows; what the benchmark cannot do is generalise it, because it was not designed to test acceleration and varies neither the galaxy nor the SMA grid.

    It is worth being exact about what the comparison supports, since the temptation is to compare the two intervals by eye. Comparing marginal spreads would be the wrong test anyway — the two ratios are measured on the same sessions, so they are paired, and the paired difference is the quantity with any power. That difference is positive in 18 of 18 sessions with a median of \(+0.07\), the smallest of them \(+0.04\). That is evidence of mild acceleration for this fixture on this machine; an unbroken sweep is not something to wave away. What it does not establish is a general scaling law across galaxies, configurations or hardware — one galaxy and one SMA grid cannot separate a property of the algorithm from a property of the fixture, and this benchmark varies neither. (Those figures are archived alongside the rest, so they are checked rather than transcribed.) Establishing the general claim would need an experiment built for it: several fixtures, a pre-registered paired criterion, and enough sessions for the outliers to stop dominating. (An earlier version of this paragraph asserted that the medians differ by less than either spread. They do not — the difference, \(0.077\), exceeds both IQR widths, \(0.037\) and \(0.038\) — and the comparison was the wrong one to make regardless.) And it does not attribute the growth to the sampling term, because the benchmark measures totals only — it does not partition runtime into sampling, solving and per-iteration overhead. A component profile would settle both questions and has not been run. What the table does establish is the distinction the \(4\times\) claim missed: rings and sampled points scale differently, and only the latter is proportional to \(a_\mathrm{max}\). That \(4\times\) figure would be right under linear SMA stepping, where the ring count itself grows in proportion.

    A caution about this measurement, because getting it wrong is easy and we did. An earlier version used a compact fixture on which six of the seven rings beyond 200 px failed with stop code \(-1\) after two or three iterations. Those rings are cheap precisely because they fail, and the resulting \(1.16\times\) was an artefact of early termination rather than evidence about sampling cost. Any scaling measurement of this kind has to report its stop codes.

    We do not quote a per-galaxy runtime floor or a survey-scale extrapolation here. Both depend on image size, SMA grid, galaxy extent and configuration in ways a single number would misrepresent, and the benchmark chapters measure them on the actual campaign samples.

    Measurement protocol. Every timing in this chapter — the lazy-gradient comparison of §1.3.2, the eccentric-anomaly and ISOFIT table of §1.4.1.4, the cold-start figures above and the two tables above — is produced by benchmarks/draft_timings/run_draft_timings.py, committed alongside the source, as are the two measurements there that are not timings: the lazy-gradient accuracy table of §1.3.2 and the NumPy-fallback break-even above. Six properties of that harness are what make the numbers worth quoting:

    • Batching. Sub-millisecond operations are repeated to a floor duration before the clock is read, because timing such a call individually measures the timer as much as the work.
    • Interleaving with a shuffled order. Repetitions of the arms being compared are interleaved rather than run in blocks. The order within each repetition is reshuffled from a recorded seed, because a fixed visiting order leaves whichever arm always runs last absorbing any within-repetition drift — which is precisely the high-band solve. Adding the shuffle measurably tightened the lazy-gradient ratio.
    • Spread, not just a median. Every raw repetition is retained and an interquartile range is reported beside each median. Some of these ratios are noisy enough that a single median would invite false precision, and the draft says so where that is the case.
    • Sessions in separate processes. A single session shares its Python imports, module state, warmed allocators and arm ordering, so repeating blocks within one understates the variability. --sessions N fans out N interpreters, each with its own arm-ordering seed, and archives every one. The chapter's aggregates come from an eighteen-session run, and they are reported as a median with an interquartile range rather than as a minimum and a maximum. That choice is empirical: across independent sets the medians and quartiles reproduce to within about a percentage point, while the extremes do not reproduce at all — one session in eighteen returned a negative lazy-gradient saving, an outlier we did not diagnose. An earlier version of this harness repeated passes inside one process and quoted min-to-max from three of them, which made its intervals look far tighter than they were and required revising them three times. Process isolation has limits worth naming: the operating system's filesystem page cache is machine-wide, and so is the machine's thermal and load state, so what separate sessions vary is process state, not the environment the process runs in.
    • Two exemptions, stated. Two blocks run in child processes because what they measure is fixed at interpreter start, so neither can be batched: cold start (three independent empty-cache/populated-cache pairs, quoted to one significant figure) and the NumPy-fallback comparison, which selects the fallback from the environment. The accuracy sweep behind the §1.3.2 table is the other kind of exemption: it measures a difference between two converged fits rather than a duration, so batching and interleaving have nothing to act on, and the quantity is fixed once the image is. What varies there is the noise realisation, so each session draws its own and the reported spread is a spread over realisations. Every other block follows the points above.
    • Two fixtures. A compact seeded Sérsic (\(n = 2\), \(R_e = 30\,\mathrm{px}\), \(\varepsilon = 0.4\), \(S/N = 50\)) for the whole-fit comparisons, and a wider, higher-\(S/N\) one for the maxsma block, which needs every ring out to 400 px to converge. The accuracy sweep reuses the compact fixture with its noise level varied, so its middle row and the lazy-gradient timing describe the same galaxy.

    The environment (Python, NumPy, Numba, BLAS) is recorded with the results. Absolute times are machine-specific and should not be compared across hardware — an independent reviewer reproduced the band-scaling ratios on different hardware while the absolute times differed by about a factor of two. The ratios are what the arguments in this chapter rest on.

    Every harness-derived timing quoted in this chapter comes from one archived run, committed as benchmarks/draft_timings/reference_timings.json, so those figures have a fixed provenance rather than depending on whichever invocation last overwrote the scratch output. A companion script, check_draft_numbers.py, rebuilds each quoted table row and summary clause from that archive and asserts the prose contains it verbatim, so a table that drifts out of step fails loudly instead of silently looking measured.

    To be precise about the scope: what the checker guards is everything the harness produces — the tables and their summary clauses in §1.3.2, §1.4.1.4 and §1.6.5, including the lazy-gradient accuracy table and the restatement of its worst case in the limitations list above. Other numbers in this chapter come from elsewhere (configuration values, cross-references, and results from the benchmark campaigns) and the checker does not speak for them; its --self-test mode lists them so the boundary is visible rather than assumed.

    Within that scope the checker matches whole rows and whole clauses rather than fragments, and --self-test perturbs each quoted number individually, requiring at least one check to fail for every one. Both properties exist because earlier versions lacked them. The original checker validated an interquartile range by searching for the bare substrings 23 and 25, which occur throughout the prose, and so passed against a draft whose figures had been deliberately altered; its successor mutated all digits simultaneously, which demonstrates only that each implemented check is sensitive to something, not that a claim has a check at all. A verification script that has never been shown to fail is not evidence.

    §1.6.6 Roadmap

    The development directions we anticipate addressing in subsequent releases, in approximate order of priority:

    GPU acceleration of hot kernels. The §1.3.2 inner kernels — design-matrix construction (single-band and joint multi-band), ellipse-coordinate construction, harmonic-model evaluation — are natural candidates for GPU acceleration through CuPy, JAX, or a vectorized PyTorch backend. The vectorized sampling step (scipy.ndimage.map_coordinates) has analogous GPU implementations available. We have not prototyped this and offer no speedup estimate: the achievable gain depends on whether the per-SMA loop can be restructured to give the device enough work per launch, which is the open design question rather than a detail. The kernels themselves are small, and a naive port that keeps the current one-isophote-at-a-time control flow would be dominated by launch overhead; the interesting version fuses the invocations across SMAs and across galaxies in a batch.

    The motivation for the GPU roadmap is survey scale. LSST-class photometric surveys with \(\mathrm{O}(10^6)\) resolved galaxies per visit deliver more data than CPU-bound ISOSTER can comfortably process in real time. The GPU path makes the per-galaxy cost low enough that survey-pipeline integration is mechanical rather than performance-engineering.

    Spectral / IFU cube support. The per-isophote loop generalizes naturally to a per-(isophote, wavelength) loop on a 3-D \((x, y, \lambda)\) cube. The wavelength axis enters through the sampling stack (sampling needs to interpolate along \(\lambda\) in addition to \((x, y)\)) and through the output schema (the results dictionary needs a wavelength dimension). The single-band sampling stack already encapsulates the implementation that the cube extension would build on, so the extension is a generalization rather than a rewrite. We anticipate IFU support landing in a paper-II release alongside applications to MaNGA, SAMI, and Mauve cubes.

    Tighter PSF-aware modes. A PSF-aware fitting mode would deconvolve the PSF from the image before isophote sampling, or equivalently convolve the harmonic-fit model with the PSF before comparison to the observed isophote. The former requires deconvolution machinery; the latter requires a forward-model generalization of the harmonic decomposition. The forward-model approach is more numerically stable and is the direction we anticipate pursuing. Initial development targets the small-galaxy regime where the PSF FWHM is a non-trivial fraction of the galaxy's effective radius; the large-galaxy regime with extended PSF wings remains an upstream-pipeline problem.

    Cross-tool QA comparison plumbing. The AutoProf-coefficient-scale porting gap mentioned in §1.4.6.2 and §1.5.3 is a known item in the cross-tool comparison plumbing. The first step is not a fix but a measurement: run AutoProf against a planted deviation and compare its reported coefficients with the ISOSTER and photutils values on the same fixture. That determines whether any conversion is needed at all — the benchmark adapter currently passes AutoProf's columns through unconverted, which presumes none is. If a conversion turns out to be required, an explicit factor in the QA plotting code is the planned approach; either way the §1.5 matrix's "unverified" entries for AutoProf can then be resolved.

    Survey-blind LSB defaults. The §1.6.2 calibration items will be tightened as the validation sample grows. The aim is a single recommended default for each LSB-regime parameter that works acceptably across HSC, DECaLS, BASS / MzLS, DES, and the eventual LSST main survey, with documented deviations only for genuinely exceptional surveys.

    A handful of smaller items — better error reporting on configuration mistakes, an interactive Jupyter QA panel, finer-grained logging — are also on the roadmap but are operational improvements rather than algorithmic ones; we do not itemize them here.

    §1.6.7 Closing remarks on the technical chapter

    The technical chapter began with a three-goal framing — speed, flexibility, scientific power — and traversed §1.1 through §1.6 demonstrating how ISOSTER meets each. The algorithmic-speed argument of §1.3 establishes the first goal at the level of operation counts and code-path organization. The configurability surface of §1.2 and the six feature subsections of §1.4 establish the second goal by exposing each algorithmic feature as a flag on a validated configuration object — IsosterConfig for single-band work and the deliberately separate IsosterConfigMB for the multi-band path. The scientific-power argument runs through the same six §1.4 subsections, each of which demonstrates one new capability the classical Python predecessors do not jointly provide: eccentric-anomaly sampling and joint ISOFIT harmonic fitting faithfully reproduced from Ciambur 2015; variance-aware fitting with gradient errors propagated from the supplied noise model rather than from residual scatter; a soft + hard composition for the LSB outskirt regime; operations-grade batch robustness primitives; joint multi-band free fitting that offers an alternative to forced photometry for colour-gradient science, with a measured precision advantage at low signal-to-noise; and a QA / reproducibility infrastructure that ties the fit outputs to the configuration that produced them through round-trip serialization.

    The aggregate is a tool calibrated for the next generation of deep, wide-field, multi-band imaging surveys — HSC-SSP, the late-stage DECaLS / BASS / MzLS samples, DES, the eventual LSST main survey — and structured so that the surveys' principal challenges (LSB outskirts, multi-band joint fitting, throughput at sample scale, reproducibility across pipeline runs) are directly addressed at the algorithmic level. The benchmark chapters that follow this technical chapter quantify the actual performance on the Huang2013, S4G, and SGA-2020 campaigns and provide the empirical evidence behind the §1.3 and §1.5 qualitative claims.

    The closing observation is that ISOSTER is not finished — the §1.6 roadmap names several substantial development directions that the next release cycles will address — but the version documented in this chapter is operational in the sense that all six §1.4 features ship with regression-test coverage, all configurations are validated at construction, and the two on-disk serialization paths of §1.4.6.3 round-trip the isophote table, the resolved configuration and the run metadata. (The third path, the Astropy-Table conversion, deliberately returns the isophote table alone and is an in-memory convenience rather than an archival format.) Users can deploy the current version on their survey pipelines today; the roadmap items are improvements to that operational baseline, not blockers to it.

    The technical chapter ends here. The benchmark chapters that follow apply the framework of §1.4.6.4 to the three campaign datasets and report the quantitative cross-tool comparisons that this chapter has carefully avoided.
