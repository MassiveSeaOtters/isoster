    §1.4.6 Diagnostics, QA, and reproducibility infrastructure

    The features described in §1.4.1 through §1.4.5 modify what ISOSTER computes. The features in the present subsection support how those computations are inspected, validated, and reproduced — they do not change the algorithm itself but they make every fit the algorithm produces traceable, comparable, and replayable. None of these features is novel in the sense of replacing a missing piece of the classical algorithm; rather, they are the operational infrastructure that distinguishes a research code from a research tool, and they are part of the technical-section discussion because survey-pipeline use of ISOSTER will rest on them at least as much as on any algorithmic feature.

    Four pieces of infrastructure are worth describing: the stop-code domain that uniformly summarizes per-isophote outcomes; the QA plotting subsystem that includes a cross-tool comparison facility for ISOSTER, photutils.isophote, and AutoProf; the serialization layer whose two on-disk formats share a common configuration round-trip property, alongside an in-memory table conversion; and the exhausted-benchmark framework that drives the cross-tool benchmark chapters of this paper.

    §1.4.6.1 Stop codes as a per-isophote outcome summary

    Every isophote in an ISOSTER output dictionary carries a stop_code field drawn from \(\{0, 1, 2, 3, -1\}\). The five values encode the five algorithmically distinct outcomes the per-isophote fit loop can produce:

    Code Meaning Trigger
    \(0\) Success Converged: \(\lvert a_\max\rvert < c \cdot S(a) \cdot \sigma_\mathrm{eff}\) with \(i \ge \mathtt{minit}\)
    \(1\) Too many flagged samples \(\mathtt{actual\_points} < \mathtt{total\_points} \cdot (1 - \mathtt{fflag})\)
    \(2\) Max-iteration fallback Reached \(\mathtt{maxit}\); best-so-far geometry returned
    \(3\) Too few points Fewer than 6 valid samples for the five-parameter harmonic fit
    \(-1\) Gradient failure Outward-only two-strike rule on gradient diagnostics, or \(\widehat{dI/da} = 0\)

    Here \(S(a)\) is the SMA-dependent convergence-scale factor of §1.1 (convergence_scaling, default 'sector_area': \(\max(1,\ a \cdot \Delta a \cdot \Delta\theta)\)), and \(\sigma_\mathrm{eff}\) is the residual RMS, floored at \(\sigma_\mathrm{bg}/\sqrt{N}\) when sigma_bg is supplied.

    The codes are not interchangeable. A stop code of \(0\) indicates that the isophote met the convergence criterion and that the reported geometry is the converged geometry. A stop code of \(2\) indicates that the iteration count was exhausted; the reported geometry is the best so far (the iteration with the smallest effective amplitude — §1.1), which is usually within a small fraction of the convergence threshold but not necessarily there. Codes \(1\) and \(3\) indicate sample-population problems — too many masked or sigma-clipped pixels, or too few valid samples for the linear system — and the fit returns a geometry that is the best attempt under degraded conditions but does not carry the statistical guarantees of a code-\(0\) converged fit. Code \(-1\) indicates a gradient-related failure that terminated the fit attempt without producing a usable geometry.

    The driver applies a uniform policy across the regular-mode growth loops: codes \(\{0, 1, 2\}\) are acceptable and propagate as the starting geometry for the next SMA step; codes \(\{3, -1\}\) are unacceptable — the failed isophote's geometry is not propagated (in the default strict mode; permissive_geometry relaxes this), but growth itself continues to the configured radius limit. The acceptable set is exposed as the module-level constant ACCEPTABLE_STOP_CODES. This policy is identical between the inward and outward loops. (Template-forced mode does not consult stop codes at all: extraction proceeds at every template SMA.) A consumer of the output dictionary can filter isophotes by stop code with a single rule:

    good   = [iso for iso in results["isophotes"] if iso["stop_code"] == 0]
usable = [iso for iso in results["isophotes"] if iso["stop_code"] in {0, 1, 2}]
failed = [iso for iso in results["isophotes"] if iso["stop_code"] < 0]

    The same stop-code domain applies to the multi-band joint fit (§1.4.5), where each isophote carries one shared stop code derived from the joint-fit convergence rather than per-band stop codes; the multi-band stop code reports whether the shared geometry converged, not whether any one band's intensity extraction succeeded.

    A property worth flagging: the stop-code domain is closed under the §1.4 features. None of the new features in this section adds new stop codes, and none of them changes the trigger for an existing stop code. The LSB auto-lock of §1.4.3 commits to a locked clone after detecting a gradient-failure trend, but the locked-clone isophotes themselves still report stop codes from the same five-value domain (typically code \(0\) since the geometry is fixed and the fit reduces to a forced extraction). The variance- aware WLS path of §1.4.2 changes neither the code-\(0\) convergence criterion nor the value of \(\sigma_\mathrm{eff}\) that enters it: in single-band, \(\sigma_\mathrm{eff}\) is the unweighted standard deviation of the fit residuals \(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) on both the OLS and the WLS path. The closure is intentional: a consumer of the results dictionary can rely on the stop-code domain being a stable contract across the configuration surface.

    One deliberate asymmetry belongs here rather than buried in §1.4.5. The multi-band joint fit does not inherit the unweighted convergence scatter. Its stopping comparison is variance-weighted, using the same weights the joint solve applied to each band's rows. The reason is that a multi-band residual pools bands whose flux units are arbitrary and unrelated: comparing a weighted amplitude against an unweighted pooled scatter makes the stopping decision move when a band is rescaled, which is not a property a convergence criterion may have. Single-band has no such exposure, since there is only one set of units, so it keeps the classical unweighted form. The stop-code domain is identical in both; only the quantity compared against the threshold differs.

    §1.4.6.2 QA plotting and cross-tool comparison

    ISOSTER ships with a QA plotting subsystem in isoster/plotting.py that takes one or more results dictionaries and produces a standardized multi-panel figure summarizing the fit. Two public entry points are exposed.

    The first, plot_qa_summary, takes a single results dictionary and the corresponding image and renders a two-column figure. The left column carries the image panels — the original image with selected isophote overlays, the reconstructed 2-D model with its iso-brightness contours, and the residual image built from that same model. The right column carries a stack of radial-profile panels sharing one \(x\) axis: the surface-brightness profile with uncertainties, the centroid drift, the axis ratio, the position angle, and — when the corresponding blocks are present — the higher-order harmonics in their Bender-normalized form and the curve of growth. The panel count is therefore not fixed; the conditional panels appear only when the fit produced the data they display. Radial panels use \(a^{1/4}\) as the abscissa, which compresses the outskirts while retaining inner-region resolution, and stop-code markers colour the profile points so that a degraded isophote is visible in the figure rather than only in the table. This is the standard single-galaxy inspection figure, and it is deliberately built from the persisted fit products — the same profile table and residual model that feed the benchmark metrics — rather than recomputed for plotting, so that what a reader inspects is what was scored.

    The second, plot_comparison_qa_figure, takes two or three results dictionaries — typically one each from ISOSTER, photutils.isophote, and AutoProf — and produces the same six-panel layout with each panel showing all methods overlaid in a color-consistent style. The function auto-detects the layout mode (solo, 1-vs-1, or three-way) from the number of supplied dictionaries, applies cross-method position-angle anchoring (to ensure the three methods' PA estimates are reported on a common branch of the \([0, \pi)\) ambiguity), and applies the per-method visual style from a small METHOD_STYLES table at the top of plotting.py so the visual conventions are consistent across the figure. This is the figure that drives the visual comparison studies in the cross-tool benchmark chapters.

    A subtlety worth flagging is the harmonic-coefficient convention dispatch. Different tools report different higher-harmonic quantities: ISOSTER in φ-mode reports image-azimuth-space coefficients; ISOSTER in ψ-mode reports eccentric-anomaly-space coefficients; photutils.isophote samples and fits its harmonics in the image polar angle φ — the same basis as ISOSTER's default φ-mode — with coefficients normalized by \(a \cdot |dI/da|\); for AutoProf the basis and normalization are unverified — the benchmark adapter passes its coefficients through unconverted, which presumes they already match, and that presumption has never been tested against a planted deviation. The QA plotting code therefore applies a per-tool dispatch on the harmonic-contour overlay step: ISOSTER φ-mode contours are drawn in image-azimuth space, ISOSTER ψ-mode contours are drawn in eccentric-anomaly space, photutils.isophote contours are currently routed through the eccentric-anomaly helper as a first-order approximation (the polar-angle and eccentric-anomaly bases coincide to first order in the ellipticity), and AutoProf contours fall back to pure ellipses without higher-order shape correction until its coefficient scale is matched. The dispatch is documented in METHOD_STYLES and is visible to the figure consumer through a legend annotation.

    Every plotted and scored harmonic is normalized, but the exact expression differs between the storage and plot-time paths and it is worth being precise. compute_deviations stores \(a_n = A_n^\mathrm{raw} / (a\,\lvert dI/da\rvert)\), using the absolute gradient; the plot-time helper _normalize_harmonic_for_plot applies the signed Bender form \(-A_n^\mathrm{raw} / (a\,dI/da)\). The two coincide wherever the gradient is negative, which is the normal outward-declining case, and differ in sign on the rare positive-gradient row. Either way the motivation is the convention of §1.1: raw \(A_n^\mathrm{raw}\) values are not directly interpretable because their magnitudes scale with the local intensity profile.

    Where the normalization is applied depends on the storage path, and this is worth stating precisely because normalizing twice is an easy and near-invisible error:

    Path Stored Normalized
    Single-band ISOSTER already normalized in compute_deviations, at fit time
    Multi-band, independent higher harmonics already normalized at fit time, per band
    Multi-band, shared raw and band-distinct: one shared dimensionless shape reconstructed into each band's own units via \(-a\,\mathrm{grad}_b\) at plot time, per band, using that band's own gradient — they all normalize back to the one shape
    Multi-band, simultaneous_* raw and identical across bands, i.e. a shared raw residual rather than a shared shape at plot time, per band; because the raw value is the same in every band, the normalized curves separate once the gradients differ. This is one of the two reasons these modes stay experimental
    Benchmark audit (scenario_summary.py) consumes the stored columns already normalized; no further division

    So the correct statement is normalize exactly once, at whichever point the path in question has not already done it — not "uniformly at plot time", which an earlier version of this section claimed and which would double-normalize three of the four rows above. The multi-band shared modes are the reason the plot-time path exists at all: they carry one raw coefficient for every band, and normalizing per band with each band's own gradient is what turns it back into band-distinct curves.

    Cross-tool comparability follows only when the tools agree on both the basis and the normalization. ISOSTER and photutils do. For AutoProf the answer is not currently established. The benchmark adapter copies its harmonic columns through unconverted, which assumes they already share the convention; that assumption has never been tested. Nothing here is a measurement in either direction, so the honest status is ignorance rather than a known mismatch. Settling it requires running AutoProf against a planted deviation and comparing with the ISOSTER and photutils values on the same fixture. Until that is done AutoProf's harmonic curves are excluded from published cross-tool comparisons and are labelled rather than silently overlaid as if commensurable.

    A build_method_profile helper standardizes the input formats: some tools deliver isophote lists, others deliver array dicts, and the comparison figure needs a common profile format. The helper absorbs the format conversion and lets the figure-construction code operate on a single canonical representation.

    §1.4.6.3 Reproducible serialization

    ISOSTER provides three serialization paths for results dictionaries. The two on-disk paths share the property that the saved file records the complete resolved configuration — no out-of-band configuration is needed to know what settings produced the fit. The property is enforced through a common helper layer in isoster/_shared.py that serializes the resolved IsosterConfig into a JSON-encoded \((\mathtt{PARAM}, \mathtt{VALUE})\) table.

    The scope of that claim is narrower than "the fit can be re-executed from the file", and the difference matters for anyone relying on it. The file carries the configuration and the results; it does not carry the experiment. The input image, the mask, the variance map, the ISOSTER version, and the numerical environment (the BLAS and LAPACK build, whether the Numba kernels were available) are all outside it. Reproducing a fit means pairing the saved configuration with those inputs, supplied separately; what the file removes is the possibility of getting the settings wrong, including by silent drift in a default between releases.

    The FITS path, isophote_results_to_fits, writes a four-HDU file: a PrimaryHDU (empty), a BinTableHDU named ISOPHOTES with one row per isophote, a BinTableHDU named CONFIG with the two-column \((\mathtt{PARAM}, \mathtt{VALUE})\) layout carrying the full configuration, and a BinTableHDU named META in the same two-column layout carrying the top-level result keys that are neither per-isophote quantities nor configuration fields — LSB lock state, first-isophote retry outcomes, outer-region reference geometry. The CONFIG HDU is the key to the round-trip property: every field of the IsosterConfig object that produced the fit is recorded, with no reliance on IsosterConfig's default values. A future ISOSTER release that adjusts a default value cannot silently change the meaning of an existing FITS file, because the file carries the explicit value that was in effect at write time. META closes the remaining gap: without it, the information describing what the driver decided during the fit would be lost on the round trip even though the configuration survived.

    The ASDF path, isophote_results_to_asdf, performs the same serialization in ASDF's tree format, which preserves native Python types (in particular, nested dicts and numpy arrays) more transparently than FITS. For the multi-band schema (§1.4.5), the per-band column proliferation reads more naturally in ASDF's tree than as flat FITS columns, and the ASDF writer is the recommended multi-band path.

    The third path, isophote_results_to_astropy_tables, returns the isophote list as an astropy.table.Table without writing to disk. This is the API path for downstream analysis code that wants the isophote data without round-tripping through a file. The table carries the same column set as the FITS ISOPHOTES HDU; the config field is dropped, since an in-memory results dictionary already carries the resolved IsosterConfig object.

    All three readers (isophote_results_from_fits, isophote_results_from_asdf, and the analogous multi-band variants) tolerate legacy files where the CONFIG block is absent or was written as FITS header keywords (earlier ISOSTER releases): the isophote table is read normally and config comes back as None — no attempt is made to reconstruct a configuration from header keywords. Within a recognized CONFIG block, the forward-compatibility convention is that fields known to the current release are honored and unknown fields (written by a newer release) are dropped, so an older ISOSTER install can still read a newer file's configuration for the fields it understands.

    The round-trip property has a concrete benefit for the benchmark chapters of this paper. The benchmark campaigns process thousands of galaxies, store the per-galaxy fit outputs as FITS or ASDF files, and downstream analyses (composite scoring, residual model construction, cross-tool comparison) re-read those files. The guarantee that each file records the settings that produced it removes a specific class of reproducibility failures — fits being compared under subtly different default values, defaults changing between ISOSTER releases without the user noticing — that would otherwise need to be controlled externally. The remaining ingredients (the images themselves, the software version, the numerical environment) are controlled by the campaign framework of §1.4.6.4 rather than by the serialization layer.

    §1.4.6.4 The exhausted-benchmark framework

    The benchmark chapters of this paper rest on a YAML-driven campaign framework documented in docs/09-exhausted-benchmark.md and implemented in benchmarks/exhausted/. We summarize it here because several features in §1.4 are evaluated against it.

    The framework operates on the principle that benchmark configurations should be enumerable — each combination of tool, configuration, and input mock is given a YAML "arm" specification, the arms are executed independently and produce per-arm outputs in a deterministic directory layout, and the analysis-side scoring code reads from those outputs to compute per-arm and cross-tool composite scores. The deterministic layout plus the round-trip serialization property of §1.4.6.3 together mean that a benchmark arm is fully specified by its YAML and its input mock: no setting is carried implicitly. Reproducing the numbers additionally requires the ISOSTER version and the numerical environment, which the campaign framework pins separately through the locked dependency set rather than through the arm specification.

    Two scoring modes are supported. The within-tool composite score includes method-specific health terms (e.g., a per-tool stop-code penalty for ISOSTER, a per-tool convergence-iteration penalty for AutoProf) and is used for the per-tool ablation studies (which ISOSTER configuration is best on this sample). The cross-tool score includes only tool-neutral residual and runtime terms, and is used for the head-to-head comparisons of ISOSTER, photutils, and AutoProf (which tool best fits the sample under each tool's recommended configuration). The two modes use the same per-galaxy data products but different scoring functions; the separation makes the cross-tool comparisons fair by excluding terms that favor any one tool's diagnostic ontology.

    Three campaign datasets are targeted by the framework, at differing stages of completion; the benchmark chapters report what each has actually produced, and this subsection should not be read as a claim that all three have been processed end to end. They are the Huang2013 mocks of nearby elliptical galaxies (multi-Sersic models, varying resolution and noise), the S4G mocks of nearby galaxies (300 late-type-focused systems, multi-component models), and the SGA-2020 real galaxies on LegacySurvey imaging (~2,000 galaxies, real-data robustness). The campaigns are described in detail in the benchmark chapters of this paper and are not re-described here; the relevance for the present subsection is that all three campaigns use the same exhausted-benchmark framework, the same serialization formats, and the same QA plotting subsystem. A reader who wants to inspect the per-galaxy outputs of any campaign can re-read the saved fits and apply plot_qa_summary or plot_comparison_qa_figure to reproduce any QA figure in the paper. The infrastructure for inspection and the infrastructure for benchmarking are the same infrastructure.

    §1.4.6.5 Closing remarks on §1.4

    This concludes the survey of new features in §1.4. The six subsections cover what ISOSTER does differently from the classical algorithm — eccentric-anomaly sampling and ISOFIT-style joint harmonics (§1.4.1), variance-aware WLS fitting (§1.4.2), the low-surface-brightness outskirt strategy (§1.4.3), operations-grade batch robustness primitives (§1.4.4), the multi-band joint free fit (§1.4.5), and the diagnostics and reproducibility infrastructure (§1.4.6). The §1.4 algorithmic features are opt-in and inert under their disabled defaults. That is a narrower statement than "ISOSTER reduces to the classical algorithm when the flags are off", which would be false: the gradient-SNR damping and step clipping of §1.4.4 are always active, the first-isophote retry defaults to three attempts, and the sampling, convergence rule and stop-code semantics differ from the classical implementations regardless of configuration (§1.5). The diagnostics of this subsection are not an algorithmic feature at all and have no disabled state. They compose with one another with a single documented exception: the outer-region Tikhonov regularization of §1.4.3 is not wired into the wider ISOFIT solver of §1.4.1, so enabling both leaves only the selector-level half of the regularization active. ISOSTER warns at configuration time when that pair is requested.

    The features were developed in the order they are presented: §1.4.1 and §1.4.2 came first because they are direct generalizations of well-established algorithmic ideas (Ciambur 2015; classical statistical estimation theory); §1.4.3 came next to address the LSB-regime failure modes that motivated the development of ISOSTER for HSC-SSP science; §1.4.4 followed when the survey-scale deployment of ISOSTER exposed the failure modes the robustness primitives address; §1.4.5 was developed once the single-band feature set had stabilized; §1.4.6 was iterated throughout to support the benchmark and QA needs of the paper this subsection is part of. The historical order is not the recommended-reading order for users, but it is informative for understanding which features are mature (§1.4.1, §1.4.2) and which are still being refined on new data (§1.4.3's parameter defaults; §1.4.5's loose-validity calibration).

    §1.5 turns to the comparison of ISOSTER, on a feature-by-feature basis, against photutils.isophote, AutoProf, and IRAF Isofit. The comparison rests on the features described in §1.4 and on the algorithmic-speed argument of §1.3; the quantitative performance comparisons come from the benchmark chapters that follow this technical section.
