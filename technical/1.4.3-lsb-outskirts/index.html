    §1.4.3 Low-surface-brightness outskirt strategy

    The fitting of galaxy isophotes in the low-surface-brightness (LSB) regime — at radii where the galaxy intensity has dropped to within a factor of a few of the sky noise — is a different problem from the fitting of inner isophotes, even though the algorithm is nominally unchanged. Three things go wrong simultaneously. The radial intensity gradient \(dI/da\) shrinks toward the photon-noise floor, so the geometry-update step (which divides each harmonic amplitude by \(dI/da\)) becomes unstable: small numerator noise produces large denominator-amplified geometry corrections. The harmonic-fit residual RMS drops below the sky noise per sample, so the convergence criterion \(|a_\max| < c \cdot S(a) \cdot \sigma_\mathrm{eff}\) (with the SMA-dependent scale factor \(S(a)\) of §1.1) would let the solver chase noise-induced asymmetries indefinitely if not for the \(\sigma_\mathrm{bg}/\sqrt{N}\) floor of §1.1. And contaminants — faint companions, scattered light, diffuse intracluster light, a bright source's extended PSF wings — that were negligible in the inner high-\(S/N\) region now contribute fractional intensity perturbations of order unity to the isophote, biasing the geometry-update direction.

    The textbook solution is to fix the geometry — center, ellipticity, position angle — at some user-chosen SMA and let the outer isophotes be evaluated as forced photometry on that fixed geometry. This works but is harsh in two ways. The transition SMA is set by hand, which is a per-galaxy parameter that scales poorly to survey samples; and the transition is binary, with no graceful degradation between the fully-free inner fit and the fully-fixed outer extraction. ISOSTER introduces two complementary features that together replace the hand-tuned transition with an adaptive one. Outer-region center regularization is a soft mechanism: a logistic Tikhonov ramp that damps geometry updates in the outer SMA range toward an inner reference centroid, with strength that grows continuously from negligible (at \(a = a_0\)) to substantial (at \(a \gg a_0\)). It runs in every outward iteration. The automatic LSB geometry lock is a hard mechanism: a one-way state-machine triggered by per-isophote gradient diagnostics that, once committed, freezes the geometry to the last clean isophote and runs the remaining outward growth as forced extraction with a robust integrator. The two features are independent — either can be used alone — but they are designed to compose: the soft regularization runs in the pre-lock phase and is silently absorbed into the locked clone when the hard lock fires.

    This section explains both features and their composition. We focus on the algorithmic and implementation details; the empirical performance of the strategy across many galaxies belongs to the benchmark chapters.

    §1.4.3.1 Soft outer-region center regularization

    Motivation and design

    The classical algorithm has no information about where the real galaxy center is, beyond what the harmonic fit at each isophote tells it. In the inner high-\(S/N\) region this is sufficient: every isophote converges to nearly the same \((x_0, y_0)\), and the geometry is well-constrained. In the outer LSB region the same harmonic fit on a much noisier signal produces a much larger parameter covariance, and the per-isophote center estimate begins to wander. The wandering is not random — contaminants pull the center systematically toward themselves; PSF wings of a nearby bright source bias the centroid along a fixed direction; an incompletely-masked companion shifts the fitted center toward the companion's position. The outward geometry trajectory can drift by several pixels over the LSB tail and produce isophotes that no longer share a center with the inner ones.

    The soft regularization assumes that the inner isophotes, where the center is well-determined, carry the correct center information, and that the outer fit should be encouraged to remain consistent with it. The encouragement is implemented as a Tikhonov-style penalty on the geometry-update step: each step in a geometry coordinate is multiplied by a shrinkage factor that grows with SMA and with the local uncertainty of that coordinate, measured through the harmonic-to-geometry Jacobian (which scales as \(1 / (dI/da)\)). Where the radial gradient is steep and the data constrain the geometry well (the inner high-\(S/N\) region), the shrinkage is negligible; where the gradient shallows toward the photon-noise floor (the LSB regime), the shrinkage engages strongly. The mechanism has no effect on the inner isophotes and its largest effect exactly where the user is most concerned — the noise-dominated LSB tail, where unconstrained geometry corrections are denominator-amplified noise.

    The logistic ramp and the inner reference

    Two ingredients enter the soft regularization. The first is a scalar strength \(\lambda(a)\) that grows continuously with SMA:

    \[ \lambda(a) = \frac{\lambda_0}{1 + \exp\!\left(-\dfrac{a - a_\mathrm{onset}}{\Delta a_\mathrm{ramp}}\right)}, \]

    where \(\lambda_0\) is the asymptotic strength (config field outer_reg_strength, default \(2.0\), suggested LSB range 1 to 3), \(a_\mathrm{onset}\) is the SMA at which the ramp reaches half its asymptotic value (config outer_reg_sma_onset), and \(\Delta a_\mathrm{ramp}\) controls the width of the transition (config outer_reg_sma_width). The logistic shape was chosen over a step function and a linear ramp for two reasons: it is smooth (no derivative discontinuities at the transition), and it has explicit "on" and "off" tails (the ramp asymptotes to \(0\) for \(a \ll a_\mathrm{onset}\) and to \(\lambda_0\) for \(a \gg a_\mathrm{onset}\)), which makes the strength interpretable at the endpoints. The width \(\Delta a_\mathrm{ramp}\) defaults to None, in which case it is auto-computed as \(0.4 \cdot a_\mathrm{onset}\); this keeps the ramp gentle on a typical SMA grid.

    The second ingredient is the inner reference geometry. ISOSTER constructs this once, between the inward loop and the outward loop. Let \(\mathcal{I}_\mathrm{inner}\) be the set of inward isophotes plus the anchor isophote at \(a_0\) that satisfy two conditions: stop code \(\in \{0, 1, 2\}\) (acceptable for propagation) and \(a \le a_0 \cdot f_\mathrm{ref}\) where \(f_\mathrm{ref}\) is the config field outer_reg_ref_sma_factor (default \(2.0\)). The reference is the flux-weighted mean of their geometries:

    \[ g_\mathrm{ref} = \frac{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}_\mathrm{inner}} w_i \, g_i} {\sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}_\mathrm{inner}} w_i}, \qquad w_i = I_{0, i}, \]

    where \(g\) stands separately for each of the center and ellipticity coordinates \((x_0, y_0, \varepsilon)\) and \(I_{0,i}\) is the inner-isophote intensity. The flux weighting concentrates the reference on the high-\(S/N\) isophotes nearest the galaxy center, where the geometry is best determined. The position angle is an axis-like quantity defined mod \(\pi\), so an arithmetic mean would map PAs near \(0\) and near \(\pi\) to the spurious value \(\pi/2\); ISOSTER instead averages PA as a flux-weighted circular mean on the doubled angle \(2\,\mathrm{PA}\), falling back to the anchor PA when the resultant length is too small for the mean to be well-defined (highly scattered inner PAs).

    If no inward isophotes qualify — typically because the inward loop never converged or because no inner isophotes met the stop-code cut — the reference silently falls back to the anchor isophote geometry at \(a_0\) alone.

    Per-axis weights and the per-coordinate shrinkage factor

    Once \(\lambda(a)\) and \(g_\mathrm{ref}\) are in hand, the regularization acts on each geometry coordinate independently. Let \(J_g\) be the harmonic-to-geometry Jacobian coefficient that converts the dominant harmonic amplitude into the update of coordinate \(g\) — for example \(J = (1 - \varepsilon)/(dI/da)\) for the minor-axis center term and \(J = 2(1-\varepsilon)/(a \cdot dI/da)\) for the ellipticity term; every \(J_g\) scales as \(1/(dI/da)\). The shrinkage factor is

    \[ \alpha(g, a) = \frac{\lambda(a) \, w_g \, J_g^2}{1 + \lambda(a) \, w_g \, J_g^2}, \]

    with \(w_g\) the per-axis weight from outer_reg_weights (three keys — "center", covering both center axes, "eps", and "pa" — default \(\{1, 1, 1\}\); a weight of zero disables the regularization on that axis entirely). Note what drives \(\alpha\): not the size of the proposed step, which does not enter the formula at all, but the shallowness of the local radial gradient. On a steep inner profile \(|J_g|\) is small and \(\alpha \to 0\); in the LSB regime \(dI/da\) approaches the noise floor, \(|J_g|\) grows, and \(\alpha \to 1\). The damping therefore engages exactly in the regime it was built for. The shrunk update under outer_reg_mode = 'damping' (default) is

    \[ \Delta g_\mathrm{damped} = (1 - \alpha) \, \Delta g, \]

    which leaves the direction of the update unchanged but reduces its magnitude. The closed form has the convenient property that \(\alpha \in [0, 1)\) identically, so the damped update never overshoots the original step direction.

    The per-axis weights are a binary switch, not a dial — and this is worth stating because the formula invites the opposite reading. \(\alpha\) is a saturating function of \(\lambda w_g J_g^2\), and in the LSB regime that product is enormous. On a real BCG test case (PGC006669), the per-axis Jacobian \(J = (1 - \varepsilon)/(dI/da)\) reaches \(\sim\!700\) in the outer LSB, because the denominator is heading toward zero; \(J^2 \approx 5 \times 10^5\), so \(\lambda w_g J_g^2 \gg 1\) for any weight above roughly \(10^{-4}\). A strength × weights sweep confirmed the consequence directly: reducing the eps and pa weights from \(1.0\) to \(0.25\) produced essentially identical outer-region pinning, with \(\alpha\) saturating above \(99.99\%\) across the whole user-intuition range. Intermediate values such as \(\{1, 0.5, 0.5\}\) are not a middle ground; they behave like \(\{1, 1, 1\}\). An honest "partial damping" regime would require \(w \sim 10^{-4}\), far below any value a user would think to type. The practical rule is therefore that each axis is either pinned (\(w_g > 0\)) or free (\(w_g = 0\)), and the choice should be made on whether that axis's outer geometry is expected to evolve.

    This is also why the single-band default \(\{1, 1, 1\}\) and the multi-band default \(\{1, 0, 0\}\) differ. Pinning ellipticity and position angle is right for a confirmed-static outer envelope — a cD galaxy, an isolated massive elliptical — and wrong for anything whose outer disc geometry might genuinely change with radius: barred and spiral systems, and the BCG-to-ICL transition. The multi-band path carries the more conservative default because the joint solver already supplies cross-band stability on ellipticity and position angle through the shared harmonic constraint, making that damping largely redundant there.

    Under outer_reg_mode = 'solver', the regularization additionally pulls the geometry toward the reference. The effective update becomes

    \[ \Delta g_\mathrm{solver} = (1 - \alpha) \, \Delta g + \alpha \, (g_\mathrm{ref} - g), \]

    i.e. a convex combination of the proposed step and a step toward the inner reference, weighted by the same shrinkage factor. The 'solver' mode is more aggressive in the sense that it actively moves the geometry back toward the inner reference even when the proposed update happens to be zero; the 'damping' mode is purely a shrinkage and leaves the equilibrium where the iteration would otherwise have placed it. For most science cases the 'damping' mode suffices; the 'solver' mode is reserved for the most demanding contamination scenarios where the LSB tail is being actively pulled away from the inner geometry.

    Selector-level penalty

    A complementary mechanism operates one level higher. ISOSTER's fit loop tracks not only the per-iteration converged geometry but also a best-iteration candidate via the effective amplitude \(a_\mathrm{eff} = |a_\max| + \mathcal{R}(g)\) (§1.1). The regularization contributes to \(\mathcal{R}\) a penalty term

    \[ \mathcal{R}_\mathrm{outer}(g, a) = \lambda(a) \sum_{g\text{-axes}} w_g \, (g - g_\mathrm{ref})^2, \]

    so that, when multiple iterations have produced candidate geometries with similar \(|a_\max|\), the iteration whose geometry is closest to the inner reference is preferred. This is a soft-tie-breaking effect; the iteration that minimizes the bare harmonic amplitude usually also minimizes the effective amplitude, because the harmonic-amplitude term dominates in the high-\(S/N\) regime. In the LSB regime the two terms become comparable, and the regularization penalty bounds the cumulative drift through the best-iteration selector even when the per-iteration shrinkage factor would by itself permit larger excursions.

    §1.4.3.2 Hard automatic LSB geometry lock

    The soft regularization is a gentle mechanism that scales with SMA and step size; it does not enforce a hard upper bound on how far the geometry can drift. For deep enough LSB tails the soft regularization is insufficient on its own, and the algorithm needs a hard mechanism that freezes the geometry once the per-isophote diagnostics indicate that the fit has crossed into a regime where free fitting is no longer scientifically meaningful.

    Trigger detector and debouncing

    The detector runs after each outward isophote has converged (or failed to converge) and inspects three per-isophote quantities: the radial gradient \(\widehat{dI/da}\), the gradient uncertainty \(\sigma_{dI/da}\), and the stop code. The relative gradient error

    \[ \rho = \frac{\sigma_{dI/da}}{\left|\widehat{dI/da}\right|} \]

    is the primary trigger. The detector flags the isophote as triggered when \(\rho\) exceeds a user-configurable threshold \(\rho_\mathrm{trig}\) (config field lsb_auto_lock_maxgerr, default \(0.30\)), when the gradient is non-negative (\(\widehat{dI/da} \ge 0\) — an unphysical flat or rising profile at this point in the fit), or when the isophote ended with stop code \(-1\) (a gradient-related failure). The threshold \(\rho_\mathrm{trig} = 0.30\) is deliberately tighter than the per-isophote gradient acceptability threshold \(\rho_\mathrm{accept} = 0.50\) (config maxgerr) that produces stop code \(-1\) on its own. This is the key design choice: the lock detector fires before the gradient checks would have terminated the outward growth, so the lock commits while there are still clean isophotes upstream to anchor to.

    Single isolated triggers are not sufficient to commit the lock. A faint companion intersected by a single isophote, an interpolation artefact along one ellipse, or a transient noise spike can produce a one-off elevated \(\rho\) without indicating that the LSB transition has actually been reached. The detector therefore debounces the trigger: \(N_\mathrm{deb}\) consecutive outward isophotes (config field lsb_auto_lock_debounce, default \(2\)) must all be triggered for the lock to commit. A clean isophote appearing in the streak resets the counter to zero. The debounce default of \(2\) means that two consecutive bad isophotes are required, which empirically distinguishes a genuine LSB transition (where the gradient diagnostics break down monotonically as \(a\) grows) from isolated isophote failures.

    Anchor selection and the locked clone

    When the debounce streak reaches \(N_\mathrm{deb}\), the lock commits. The locked-anchor isophote is not the most recent triggered isophote (whose geometry may already have drifted); it is the last clean isophote before the streak began. Concretely, if the streak occupies indices \(k, k+1, \ldots, k + N_\mathrm{deb} - 1\) in the outward growth list, the anchor is isophote \(k - 1\). Its geometry \((x_0^\star, y_0^\star, \varepsilon^\star, \mathrm{PA}^\star)\) is the geometry that will be enforced on every subsequent outward isophote.

    The lock is implemented as a cloned configuration. ISOSTER calls

    locked_config = config.model_copy(update=dict(
    fix_center=True, fix_pa=True, fix_eps=True,
    integrator=config.lsb_auto_lock_integrator,
))

    and the subsequent outward isophotes are fit with the locked clone. Three concrete effects follow. First, the geometry-update machinery (§1.1) sees fix_center=True, fix_pa=True, fix_eps=True and zeros all four geometry coordinates' updates by zeroing the corresponding harmonic amplitudes before the dominant-amplitude selector runs. The fitted geometry is held at the anchor exactly; no iterative drift is possible. Second, the integrator switches from the default 'mean' to whatever the user-configured lsb_auto_lock_integrator is — by default 'median', which is more robust to the asymmetric residual distributions typical of LSB-regime contamination. Third, the lock is one-way: once committed, the locked clone is used for the remainder of outward growth and the original config is never restored within the same fit_image call. The inward loop and the central pixel are unaffected because both have already completed before the outward loop begins.

    The locked isophotes carry two additional output fields. The boolean lsb_locked is set on every locked isophote so the caller can filter the output by lock state. The boolean lsb_auto_lock_anchor is set on the committing trigger isophote — the last isophote of the debounce streak, which was itself still fit free — serving as a marker in QA overlays for the exact SMA at which the lock committed. (The geometry that is enforced from the next isophote onward is that of the clean anchor isophote just before the streak, not that of the marked trigger isophote.) The top-level results dict gains four companion fields: lsb_auto_lock (echo that the feature was on), lsb_auto_lock_sma (the SMA at which the lock committed, or None if the detector never triggered), lsb_auto_lock_anchor_sma (the SMA of the true geometry anchor — the last clean isophote before the streak, whose geometry the lock enforces — or None), and lsb_auto_lock_count (the number of outward isophotes fit under the locked clone).

    Implementation prerequisites and conflict checks

    The detector reads grad, grad_error, and grad_r_error from each outward isophote's output dictionary. These fields are only present when debug=True is set in the config. ISOSTER's config validator does not require the caller to set debug=True explicitly; instead, when lsb_auto_lock=True is set with debug=False, ISOSTER emits a UserWarning and internally clones the config with debug=True for the duration of the fit. The caller's original config remains unchanged. This trade is deliberate: forcing the caller to enable a debug flag for a production feature would be confusing, and the storage overhead of the five extra fields per isophote (grad, grad_error, grad_r_error, plus the ndata / nflag sample counts) is negligible.

    The Pydantic validator enforces three hard rejection rules at config-construction time. lsb_auto_lock=True cannot coexist with fix_center=True, fix_pa=True, or fix_eps=True; the combination is rejected with ValidationError because the auto-lock is itself a mechanism for fixing the geometry, and pairing it with manual fixed-geometry flags would be either redundant or self-contradictory depending on which fix is meant to take precedence. The auto-lock is also silently inactive when combined with template-based forced photometry (template=…); a UserWarning is emitted at the top of fit_image and the feature is bypassed entirely. The reason is that template-based forced extraction already enforces fixed geometry per SMA from the template, leaving nothing for the lock to do.

    §1.4.3.3 Composition of the two mechanisms

    Both features are independent in the sense that either can be enabled without the other. They are also designed to compose: when both are on, the soft regularization runs from \(a_0\) outward and gradually damps the geometry trajectory; if the LSB tail is deep enough that the gradient diagnostics begin to break down, the hard lock then commits and freezes the geometry for the remainder.

    The composition is mechanically clean. The locked-clone config that the auto-lock installs has fix_center=True, fix_pa=True, fix_eps=True, which zeroes the geometry-update directions before the soft regularization shrinkage would have a chance to multiply them. The shrinkage factor \(\alpha\) is multiplying a zero step, so the regularization is a silent no-op for the locked tail. There is no need to disable the regularization explicitly when the lock fires; the math takes care of it.

    One composition that does not work

    The outer-region regularization and ISOFIT (§1.4.1) do not fully compose, and the limitation is worth stating rather than leaving to a runtime warning. The Tikhonov term is applied at the point where a five-parameter harmonic solve is converted into a geometry step; it was never wired into the wider ISOFIT joint solver. Setting use_outer_center_regularization=True together with simultaneous_harmonics=True therefore silently loses the solver-level shrinkage: only the selector-level penalty of the previous section still applies, so the best-iteration tie-break survives but the per-iteration step damping does not. ISOSTER emits a UserWarning naming exactly this at configuration time rather than proceeding quietly, but the combination is a genuine gap, not a design choice, and closing it is on the roadmap (§1.6).

    This is the one exception to the composability claim made elsewhere in §1.4: with that single pair, enabling both features does not give the union of their effects.

    The intended division of labor between the two mechanisms can be read off this composition. The soft regularization is a prophylactic measure that operates while the gradient diagnostics are still within tolerance: it bounds the cumulative drift in the pre-LSB regime, where the fit is technically still valid but where small contamination can pull the centroid off its inner-isophote consensus. The hard lock is a terminal measure that activates when the gradient diagnostics indicate the fit can no longer be trusted to follow the galaxy. The two failure modes the strategy is designed to address are therefore different in character: contamination-driven drift in the pre-lock regime, and noise-driven divergence in the post-lock regime. Each mechanism is targeted at one of them, and the composition produces a single ISOSTER fit that spans both regimes without a hand-tuned transition.

    A practical observation. The auto-lock's debounce default of \(2\) and the trigger threshold of \(0.30\) are calibrated so that the lock typically commits a few isophotes before the unconstrained fit would have produced stop code \(-1\). The soft regularization therefore operates over a non-zero range of SMAs before the lock fires, and that range can be substantial — tens of SMAs on a deeply-fit galaxy. The soft regularization is not a vestigial warm-up step; it is doing real work in that range.

    §1.4.3.4 Caveats

    The two features carry a small number of caveats that bear on when to enable them.

    Both features are disabled by default. The user has to opt in explicitly. ISOSTER's default fit is the classical Jedrzejewski / photutils behaviour with no LSB-regime intervention. This is the right default for fits that do not push into LSB territory; the features should be enabled when the user is fitting deep imaging where they expect contamination or noise-driven drift.

    The soft regularization is still being calibrated across surveys. The default parameter values (outer_reg_sma_onset, outer_reg_sma_width, outer_reg_strength) are heuristic recommendations; the values that work well on HSC edge cases may be sub-optimal on DECaLS, which has different seeing, sky brightness, and noise characteristics. The recommended-range values in docs/02-configuration-reference.md are starting points, not survey-blind defaults.

    The hard lock detector reads gradient diagnostics, not the image directly. It is therefore agnostic to the image content beyond what the per-isophote fit reports. A galaxy with structurally unusual outskirts (truncated disks, tidal streams) may produce gradient diagnostics that look like LSB-regime divergence even when the image data are still informative. The lock will commit in that case, and the user will need to inspect the locked tail to decide whether the freeze was appropriate. The output fields lsb_auto_lock_sma and lsb_locked make this inspection straightforward.

    The lock is one-way per fit. There is no "unlock and re-fit free" mode. If the lock commits early on a galaxy where the user believed the inner region warranted a longer free fit, the recommended workflow is to raise the trigger threshold lsb_auto_lock_maxgerr (or the debounce count lsb_auto_lock_debounce) and re-run, rather than to post-process the locked isophotes back into free isophotes. The locked isophotes are output with their fixed-geometry origin clearly marked; they should be interpreted as forced photometry, not as free-fit results.

    Forced photometry through template=… disables both features silently. This is a property of the template-based forced extraction path, which is structurally distinct from the regular iterative driver. A UserWarning is emitted at the top of fit_image to make the bypass visible. Users who need the LSB strategy on a multi-band workflow should prefer the joint multi-band free fit of §1.4.5, which does pass the LSB features through to each band's converged geometry.

    §1.4.3.5 Proposed demonstration: deep mock with bright contaminant

    To make the composition of the two features visible in isolation we use a synthetic galaxy generated through the MockGal renderer. The recipe is a single-component Sersic profile with \(n_\mathrm{Sersic} = 4.0\) (de Vaucouleurs-like), effective semi-major axis \(R_e = 50\,\mathrm{px}\), ellipticity \(\varepsilon = 0.30\), and total flux scaled to deliver a central surface brightness of \(\mu_0 \approx 17.5\,\mathrm{mag\,arcsec^{-2}}\) in a fiducial band. The image is \(1024 \times 1024\,\mathrm{px}\) with Gaussian sky noise corresponding to \(\mu_\mathrm{sky} = 27.5\,\mathrm{mag\,arcsec^{-2}}\), deep enough that the outward fit can be pushed to \(a_\mathrm{max} = 400\,\mathrm{px}\) before the gradient diagnostics start to fail. A bright companion — a point source at \(r \approx 220\,\mathrm{px}\) from the target's center, with flux roughly \(10\%\) of the target's central peak — is added to the rendered image after PSF convolution but before noise. The companion is masked out only in a small region around its nominal centroid; its PSF wings extend across a substantial range of outer SMAs and would, in an unconstrained fit, bias the centroid toward the companion's direction.

    The three fits would use the same starting geometry, the same astep, the same maxsma, and the same OLS setting, so that the only difference between the runs is the LSB-feature configuration and the drift comparison isolates each intervention. (We use OLS for clarity. Whether the picture is qualitatively the same under WLS is an open question rather than an assumption: the weights change the solve wherever the variance varies across a ring, and the LSB outskirts are where that is most likely.)

    Each run is there to answer a question rather than to illustrate a conclusion. The free fit establishes whether the contamination failure mode occurs at all on this mock, and how strongly. The soft-mode run measures how much the prophylactic bounds the drift — noting that §1.4.3.1's saturation analysis predicts the shrinkage factor is near-total in this regime, so "bounds" may understate what it does. The combined run tests whether the composition yields a single fit spanning the pre-lock and post-lock regimes without a hand-tuned transition SMA, and at what cost in the locked tail.

    The reproducibility recipe is:

    from isoster import fit_image
from isoster.config import IsosterConfig

# Free fit (baseline)
config_free = IsosterConfig(
    sma0=10.0, maxsma=400.0,
)

# Soft regularization only
config_soft = IsosterConfig(
    sma0=10.0, maxsma=400.0,
    use_outer_center_regularization=True,
    outer_reg_sma_onset=180.0,
    outer_reg_sma_width=20.0,
    outer_reg_strength=2.0,
    outer_reg_weights={"center": 1.0, "eps": 1.0, "pa": 1.0},
)

# Soft + hard, the recommended deep-LSB configuration
config_combined = IsosterConfig(
    sma0=10.0, maxsma=400.0,
    use_outer_center_regularization=True,
    outer_reg_sma_onset=180.0,
    outer_reg_sma_width=20.0,
    outer_reg_strength=2.0,
    outer_reg_weights={"center": 1.0, "eps": 1.0, "pa": 1.0},
    lsb_auto_lock=True,
    lsb_auto_lock_maxgerr=0.30,
    lsb_auto_lock_debounce=2,
    lsb_auto_lock_integrator="median",
    debug=True,
)

results_free     = fit_image(image, mask=mask, config=config_free)
results_soft     = fit_image(image, mask=mask, config=config_soft)
results_combined = fit_image(image, mask=mask, config=config_combined)

print("lock committed at sma =", results_combined["lsb_auto_lock_sma"])
print("locked isophote count =", results_combined["lsb_auto_lock_count"])

    In the configuration above, outer_reg_sma_onset = 180 places the soft-regularization ramp midpoint at the SMA where the companion's PSF wings begin to contribute appreciably to the isophote, so the soft mechanism engages where the contamination problem starts. Where the auto-lock then commits is a measurement the run would supply — the two print statements exist to report it — and whether it lands outside the ramp onset, giving the intended soft-pre-lock / hard-post-lock division of labour, is exactly what has not yet been checked.

    A closing observation. The LSB strategy is opt-in by design. On shallow surveys, on bright nearby galaxies fit only to a few effective radii, or on isolated targets with clean fields, the classical free fit is the right answer and the LSB features add nothing. The strategy is for the regime where the science case explicitly requires pushing into the LSB outskirts and where contamination or noise-driven divergence is anticipated: deep imaging surveys (HSC-SSP, the eventual LSST main survey), nearby galaxies in crowded fields, intracluster light measurements, truncation-radius studies. In those regimes the strategy quantifiably bounds the drift; in others it is a no-op, which is also the right behaviour.
