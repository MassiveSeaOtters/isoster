    §1.5 Comparison to existing tools

    This subsection situates ISOSTER among the three most directly relevant non-parametric elliptical-isophote tools: IRAF Ellipse / Isofit / Cmodel (the Jedrzejewski 1987 algorithm and its Ciambur 2015 reformulation), photutils.isophote (the direct Python re-implementation), and AutoProf (Stone, Arora, Courteau & Cuillandre 2021, the modern Python pipeline most similar in scope to ISOSTER). The comparison is conceptual: we describe each tool's design philosophy, the features it provides, and the gaps relative to ISOSTER, without quoting numerical benchmark results. Quantitative head-to-head comparisons on the Huang2013, S4G, and SGA-2020 campaigns are presented in the benchmark chapters of this paper.

    The aim is honest characterization, not advocacy. ISOSTER builds on ideas from all three predecessors; on several axes the comparison is one of generalization rather than improvement (ISOSTER offers more options at the same algorithmic core); on others it is one of new capability (variance-aware fitting, joint multi-band free fitting); on a few it is one of deferred implementation (deconvolution-aware modes, the analysis sub-systems AutoProf has accumulated over its longer deployment history). Each axis is discussed below.

    §1.5.1 vs IRAF Ellipse / Isofit / Cmodel (Jedrzejewski 1987;

    Ciambur 2015)

    The IRAF Ellipse task is the original implementation of the Jedrzejewski (1987) algorithm and remains the de facto reference against which all subsequent implementations are validated. IRAF Isofit and Cmodel are Ciambur (2015)'s extensions for eccentric-anomaly sampling and joint higher-order harmonic fitting respectively, packaged as drop-in replacements for Ellipse and its model-reconstruction counterpart Bmodel.

    ISOSTER's relationship to this family is one of faithful reimplementation with extensions. The five-parameter harmonic fit, the geometry-update rule (dominant-amplitude coordinate descent), the inward / outward growth pattern, the stop-code domain (semantically equivalent to IRAF's status codes), and the acceptable-codes propagation policy are all preserved. The ψ-basis sampling of §1.4.1 reproduces Ciambur (2015)'s Isofit construction in its use_eccentric_anomaly=True mode; the joint ISOFIT-style harmonic solve of §1.4.1 reproduces Isofit's extended design matrix in its simultaneous_harmonics=True, isofit_mode='in_loop' configuration. The post-hoc higher-order fit in the isofit_mode='original' variant reproduces the Ciambur (2015) original procedure.

    On axes the IRAF family does not address, ISOSTER provides extensions:

    • Variance-aware fitting (§1.4.2). IRAF Ellipse and Isofit use unweighted ordinary least squares for the harmonic fit. The WLS path of ISOSTER is novel relative to the IRAF family.
    • LSB outskirt strategy (§1.4.3). The IRAF family has no automatic LSB-regime mechanism; users push to whatever SMA the fit converges to and accept the failure mode beyond.
    • Multi-band joint free fit (§1.4.5). IRAF Ellipse is single-band by design; multi-band photometry requires running Ellipse independently per band and accepting the band-distinct geometries, or running an external forced-photometry workflow. ISOSTER's joint free fit is novel.
    • Operations infrastructure (§1.4.4, §1.4.6). Batch retry, permissive geometry propagation, Pydantic-validated configuration, round-trip serialization of the configuration alongside the results, ASDF support, JSON-encoded config tables — these are operational concerns the IRAF family predates.

    On axes the IRAF family does address, two practical differences matter:

    • Implementation language. The IRAF family is a Fortran / C implementation invoked through IRAF's CL scripting; ISOSTER is pure Python with vectorized NumPy / SciPy / Numba kernels. Performance differences depend on the platform; the Python re-implementations of the IRAF family (photutils.isophote, ISOSTER) generally trade IRAF's tight inner-loop performance for the Python ecosystem's data-science integration. ISOSTER recovers a substantial portion of IRAF's inner-loop performance through Numba JIT on the linear-system kernels (§1.3.2).
    • Maintenance and accessibility. IRAF is no longer actively maintained; new analyses are increasingly written in Python. The IRAF family remains the reference for cross-validation but is decreasingly the day-to-day tool.

    The IRAF family is therefore the algorithmic ancestor against which the correctness of any Python re-implementation should be checked, but the IRAF family itself is not where the practical day-to-day fits are run on contemporary survey data.

    §1.5.2 vs photutils.isophote

    The photutils.isophote package is the direct Python re-implementation of the IRAF Ellipse algorithm. It is maintained as a subpackage of photutils, the broader Astropy- ecosystem photometry library. The implementation faithfully reproduces the Jedrzejewski (1987) algorithm; it is the natural predecessor against which ISOSTER's Python-level speed and feature gains are measured, and the comparison against it is the most relevant for users coming from the Astropy ecosystem.

    ISOSTER and photutils.isophote agree on the core algorithm. Both compute the same five-parameter harmonic fit, both update geometry through the dominant-amplitude rule, both grow outward from an anchor SMA with sigma-clipping. The OLS code path of ISOSTER (i.e. with no variance map supplied) is the same ordinary least-squares solve photutils.isophote performs; the two implementations nevertheless ship different defaults (sampling count, minit, convergence details), so numerical agreement is a matched-configuration question that the benchmark chapters address rather than an out-of-the-box identity. Differences appear in three classes:

    Performance. The §1.3 algorithmic-speed argument names four choices that ISOSTER makes which photutils.isophote does not make: end-to-end vectorized sampling through map_coordinates, Numba JIT inner kernels, lazy gradient evaluation, and a shared sampling stack across regular fitting and forced extraction (2-D model reconstruction solves a different problem and does not use it). The first three are direct speed improvements at the algorithmic level; the fourth is a software- engineering improvement that compounds the first three. The benchmark chapters quantify the aggregate speedup; the qualitative argument is that ISOSTER replaces a per-pixel Python sampling loop with one C-level interpolation call per isophote, while photutils.isophote retains the per-pixel loop in the integrator classes that handle area-integral sampling.

    Algorithm extensions. ISOSTER's eccentric-anomaly sampling, ISOFIT-style joint harmonic fits, variance-aware (WLS) fitting, LSB outskirt strategy, multi-band joint free fit, and batch robustness primitives are all new relative to photutils.isophote. The latter implements the classical algorithm faithfully but does not extend it. Users who need any of the §1.4 features have no direct path through photutils.isophote and currently route through tool-specific external machinery (mask-then-mask-again loops, per-band external forced-photometry pipelines, hand-tuned transition SMAs for LSB fits).

    Operational features. ISOSTER's four-HDU FITS layout with explicit CONFIG block (§1.4.6.3), the round-trip serialization property, ASDF support, Pydantic-validated configuration with cross-parameter rejection, and the QA plotting subsystem with cross-tool comparison facility are all ISOSTER additions. photutils.isophote returns Astropy tables and leaves configuration management to the caller; QA plotting is left to the user's downstream pipeline.

    Two areas where photutils.isophote has the upper hand should be named honestly. First, photutils.isophote is part of the broader photutils package, which carries decades of accumulated photometry infrastructure (aperture photometry, source detection, PSF photometry, background estimation). A user already working in the photutils ecosystem benefits from staying within it; ISOSTER is an external dependency by comparison. Second, photutils.isophote has been deployed in published photometric analyses for longer than ISOSTER, and the consensus on its calibration and behaviour is more mature. ISOSTER's test suite does pin agreement with photutils.isophote on a panel of synthetic Sérsic models, but the scope of that check is narrow enough to be worth stating rather than gesturing at. It is an agreement band rather than an equivalence — the median intensity difference must stay under \(1\%\), the median ellipticity difference under \(0.01\), and the median position-angle difference under \(5^\circ\) — and the panel varies Sérsic index, ellipticity and noise level while holding the effective radius fixed at \(20\,\mathrm{px}\) throughout, so it does not probe the size dependence at all. The configurations are also matched by construction rather than run out of the box: the harness enables eccentric-anomaly sampling above \(\varepsilon = 0.3\) and relaxes maxgerr above \(\varepsilon = 0.6\). One further gap is worth naming because it affects the quantity most prone to it: the comparison helper differences position angles as if they were \(2\pi\)-periodic, whereas PA is an axis-like quantity defined mod \(\pi\), so galaxies near the wrap boundary are not yet robustly covered. Agreement on the medians of a fixed-size synthetic panel is a considerably weaker statement than agreement on real galaxies, and the long-tail behaviour on edge cases is still being characterized. (§1.3.4 is explicit that it makes no cross-tool numerical-agreement claim; the quantitative comparison belongs to the benchmark chapters.)

    The honest summary is narrower than "strict generalization", and the temptation to claim that should be resisted. ISOSTER implements the same algorithm family as photutils.isophote and adds a substantial feature set on top, but it is not a superset in the sense that every photutils.isophote fit is reproducible by flipping ISOSTER's flags off. The two differ in ways that no configuration switch reconciles: the sampling and integration machinery, the convergence rule and its SMA-dependent scaling, the step-clipping and gradient-SNR safeguards that are always on in ISOSTER, the stop-code semantics, and a range of defaults. Some of those differences are deliberate improvements and none is switchable back.

    Whether the two agree on a given galaxy under matched configurations is therefore an empirical question, not an identity that follows from the code structure, and the benchmark chapters treat it as such. Users who do not need the extensions have a defensible reason to remain with photutils.isophote; users who need any of the §1.4 features have a defensible reason to move; and users migrating an existing analysis should expect to validate the transition rather than assume it.

    §1.5.3 vs AutoProf (Stone, Arora, Courteau & Cuillandre 2021)

    AutoProf is the modern Python pipeline most directly comparable to ISOSTER in scope. Both tools target the same problem class (non-parametric elliptical isophote fitting on modern survey imaging, with a particular focus on the LSB regime), both are written in Python with C / vectorized kernels for the inner loops, both deploy on survey samples in the thousands, and both make explicit the LSB-regime failure modes the classical algorithm encounters and offer specific machinery for handling them. The comparison is therefore the most informative of the three for positioning ISOSTER's design choices.

    The fundamental design difference is in how the LSB-regime failure modes are addressed. AutoProf adopts an ML-style fit-stabilization approach: a regularization scheme that smooths the per-SMA geometry estimates across radii using techniques adapted from the machine-learning literature on overparameterized regression. The smoothing operates in the post-iteration analysis step, taking the per-SMA fit outputs as input and producing regularized geometry profiles that are constrained to vary smoothly with SMA. The approach is global: the regularization strength is set by a single parameter and applies uniformly across the SMA range.

    ISOSTER's LSB strategy (§1.4.3) is local and explicit: a logistic-Tikhonov ramp \(\lambda(a)\) damps geometry updates as a function of SMA, with the strength growing continuously from the high-\(S/N\) inner regime (where the damping is essentially zero) to the LSB tail (where the damping is substantial). The strength is set by three interpretable parameters (outer_reg_strength, outer_reg_sma_onset, outer_reg_sma_width) and the spatial profile of the damping is explicit. The automatic LSB lock layer (§1.4.3.2) adds a hard-threshold state-machine mechanism that freezes the geometry when the gradient diagnostics indicate the fit can no longer be trusted. Together the soft + hard pair is a designed-to-compose mechanism with no equivalent in AutoProf, but also no claim to be globally smoother than AutoProf's regularization.

    The two approaches have different scientific philosophies. The ML-style approach in AutoProf treats the per-SMA fits as noisy samples of a smooth function and uses prior information about smoothness as the regularizer. The logistic-Tikhonov approach in ISOSTER treats the per-SMA fits as iteration-trajectory points and uses an SMA-dependent prior that pulls toward an inner reference. Neither is uniformly better; they target different mental models of the underlying problem and produce different trade-offs on different galaxy classes. We anticipate the benchmark chapters will show that the two approaches agree to within their respective regularization strengths on the non-LSB-dominated subsamples and diverge most visibly on the deep-LSB-contaminated subsamples, where the failure mode the strategy is addressing actually fires.

    On axes that are not specific to the LSB strategy:

    • Variance-aware fitting. AutoProf has variance handling built into its pipeline as documented in Stone et al. 2021; AutoProf's treatment differs in implementation detail from ISOSTER's WLS formulation but achieves the analogous outcome of weighting per-pixel contributions by photon noise.
    • Eccentric-anomaly sampling. Whether AutoProf's harmonic coefficients share the ISOSTER / Bender normalization is unverified: the benchmark adapter passes them through unconverted, which presumes they do, and that presumption has never been tested. An earlier version of this chapter asserted the scale differed, which was equally untested; neither position rests on a measurement. Until one exists, AutoProf's higher-order harmonics are excluded from published cross-tool comparisons; whether any coefficient-scale conversion is needed is precisely what the verification would determine (§1.4.6.2). This is a gap in our knowledge of the interface, not an algorithmic gap in AutoProf itself.
    • Multi-band joint free fit. AutoProf operates per-band and uses external forced-photometry workflows to cross bands. The joint multi-band free fit of §1.4.5 is novel relative to AutoProf.
    • Analysis subsystem. AutoProf has a substantial analysis subsystem accumulated over its longer deployment history, including slice-based alternative profiles, smooth axisymmetric model reconstruction, and decision-tree pipelining. ISOSTER's analysis subsystem is more focused: 2-D model reconstruction from the converged harmonic decomposition, curve-of-growth attachment, and the QA plotting subsystem of §1.4.6.2. A user who needs AutoProf's analysis features will find ISOSTER less complete on those axes.
    • LSB depth. AutoProf reports reaching surface brightnesses approximately 2 magnitudes per square arcsecond fainter than classical predecessors on the same imaging (Stone et al. 2021). ISOSTER's LSB strategy is designed to reach comparable depths; the benchmark chapters quantify the actual comparison on the shared mock and real-galaxy samples.

    The honest summary is that AutoProf and ISOSTER are parallel modern pipelines that have made different design choices on several axes (LSB regularization philosophy, multi-band strategy, analysis-subsystem scope) and that overlap substantially on others (variance handling, single-band fitting on survey imaging). Users facing a choice between them should base the choice on which design philosophy matches the science target and on which analysis-subsystem features they need.

    A handful of cells deserve elaboration. The "(✓) different formulation" entry for AutoProf's variance handling reflects that AutoProf does weight per-pixel contributions but through a mechanism that differs from ISOSTER's explicit \(\mathbf{W} = \mathrm{diag}(1/\sigma_i^2)\) WLS construction; the two are operationally equivalent in the limit of well-behaved variance maps but differ in detail. The "basis unverified" and "scale unverified" entries for AutoProf's harmonics are exactly that — unverified, not established as different. The benchmark adapter copies its coefficients through without conversion, which presumes they match ours, while §1.4.6.2 has recorded the opposite. Nobody has run the experiment that would settle it, so these cells state ignorance rather than a finding, and AutoProf's harmonics stay out of published comparisons until they are checked. The "(✓) Cmodel" entry under 2-D model reconstruction for IRAF reflects that Cmodel and ISOSTER's build_isoster_model are functionally parallel; both produce a 2-D model image from the converged isophote list.

    The matrix is not a quality-ranking. Several rows where ISOSTER shows ✓ and the other tools show — reflect features that are ISOSTER's design contributions; several other rows where AutoProf shows ✓ and ISOSTER shows — reflect AutoProf's longer-deployment analysis subsystem. The two pipelines have grown in different directions from a shared algorithmic core.

    §1.5.5 Closing observations on the comparison

    Three observations close this subsection.

    The first is that ISOSTER's positioning is not "the replacement" for any of the three predecessors. The IRAF family remains the algorithmic reference and the historical canon. photutils.isophote remains the right choice for users in the Astropy ecosystem who do not need the §1.4 features. AutoProf remains the right choice for users whose science requires its specific analysis-subsystem features or whose LSB problem maps better onto the ML-style regularization philosophy. ISOSTER fills the niche of modern Python pipeline with explicit configurable LSB-regime mechanisms, joint multi-band free fit, and Pydantic- validated configuration with round-trip reproducibility — a niche that no existing tool covers in full.

    The second is that the comparison is not stable in time. Each of the three predecessors is maintained by an active community; features that ISOSTER currently provides may be added to one or more of them in future releases. The feature matrix should be read as a snapshot of the state at the time of this paper, not as a permanent characterization. We will update the matrix in subsequent revisions to reflect the moving state of the ecosystem.

    The third is that the comparison rests on a substantial body of shared algorithmic ancestry. All four tools implement the Jedrzejewski (1987) algorithm at their core; the differences are in the extensions, the implementation choices, and the operational machinery around that core. The benchmark chapters of this paper, which quantify the actual cross-tool comparison on shared datasets, will show that the four tools agree on the geometry and intensity outputs to within their respective configuration choices on a substantial fraction of galaxies, and diverge where the configuration choices and extensions become relevant. The §1.5 comparison maps the configuration-and-extension space; the benchmark chapters fill in the numbers.
